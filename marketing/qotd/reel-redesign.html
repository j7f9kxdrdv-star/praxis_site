The Ten Second Problem

Reel #1 was skipped by 68.6% of viewers and held the rest for four seconds. The instinct is to blame the hook, or the difficulty. The arithmetic says the Reel was never possible to answer.

What actually went wrong

The competitive inhibition question is 59 words once the stem and all four options are counted. At 200 words per minute, a generous rate for a phone, that is 17.7 seconds of reading. The Reel ran for ten, with a countdown draining from the first frame.

So a viewer landed on a wall of text, saw a clock running out, and understood within about a second that they could not finish. Leaving at four seconds is not a failure of interest. It is the correct response to an impossible task.

The diagnosis: the template fuses two different jobs into one panicked block. Reading wants to be calm and untimed. Deciding wants a clock. Reel #1 put the clock on both.

Every template below separates them. None of them makes the question easier.

What stays exactly as it is

The stem remains a complete MCAT-style question in natural sentences. No shorthand, no fact fragments, no arrows.

Adding compound X to an enzyme assay raises the apparent Km from 2 mM to 8 mM while Vmax is unchanged. Flooding the assay with substrate fully restores the original reaction rate. Where does X most likely bind?

One change to the typesetting, and it is in service of reading speed rather than decoration: any symbol carrying a subscript is set in the brand green at a heavier weight. Km, Vmax and kcat become anchors the eye can jump between, so the shape of the problem can be grasped before the sentence is read word by word. No highlight, no colour block, nothing that would cheapen the page.

14 seconds · recommended

The full stem is on screen from the first frame with no countdown at all, so the opening second reads as an invitation rather than a stopwatch. The four options fade in at six seconds, and only then does the clock start.

Motion

Three events only: a 0.3s fade at each end of the clip, one fade as the options arrive, and the ring depleting. The options occupy their space from the first frame at zero opacity, so the stem does not shift by a pixel when they appear. Text that jumps while it is being read costs more than any reveal gains.

Why this should cut the skip rate

Two mechanisms. First, the opening frame is no longer a countdown over a wall of text; it is a question with room to breathe, which removes the instant “I cannot do this” judgement that drove the four second exit. Second, and more structurally, the options do not exist until six seconds in. Anyone who wants to play has to stay that long. That is a floor under watch time rather than a hope.

Motion

Sentence by sentence fade. Critically, the full stem height is reserved from the first frame and only opacity changes. My first cut of this let the block grow beat by beat, and because it is vertically centred the whole stem crept upward as it built. That was worse than no reveal at all.

Why this should cut the skip rate

It attacks the first half second harder than A does. A viewer meeting template A still meets a paragraph, even a well set one. A viewer meeting B meets one line and something visibly happening. The trade is that someone landing at second four sees a partial question, which the Reel loop partly repairs but does not fully.

Why it is here at all

It is the honest baseline. It isolates one variable, the typography and the two extra seconds, so if A or B wins you know the staging did the work rather than the larger type. It should be posted third, as a check, not first.

Its weakness is that it keeps the flaw that caused the problem: 59 words still need 17.7 seconds and the clip still runs 12, with the clock visible the whole way. It is better than Reel #1 and still asks the impossible.

One caution about the numbers

Reel #1 had 74 views. That is a very small sample, and the difference between a 68.6% and a 55% skip rate at that size could easily be noise. Nothing here should be treated as proven.

What does not depend on the sample is the arithmetic: 59 words cannot be read in ten seconds by anyone. That part is certain regardless of how many people saw it. The staging fixes a real defect. Whether it moves the metric needs three or four posts per template before the comparison means anything.

Worth watching specifically: retention at three seconds separates A from B, since that is where the cascade should pull ahead. Average watch time tests A's floor, and should land near six seconds if the mechanism works at all. Poll votes per view is the only measure of whether any of this produced thinking rather than scrolling.
