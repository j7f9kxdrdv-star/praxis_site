## Q1 — Amino Acid Structure & Stereochemistry · **easy** · Skill 1 · 60s · conf 5
**Stem:** A chemist comparing the 20 proteinogenic amino acids notes that they share an identical backbone and diverge only at the side chain. In this shared backbone, the \(\alpha\)-carbon of every amino acid is bonded to which set of four groups?

- **A.** Amino group, carboxyl group, hydrogen, and R-group ✅
- **B.** Amino group, carboxyl group, and R-group only
- **C.** Amino group, phosphate group, hydrogen, and R-group
- **D.** Amino group, carboxyl group, and two R-groups

**Correct (A):** LibreTexts Biochemistry, lines 5101–5106: \"the alpha carbon is also attached to a hydrogen atom and to one additional group that can vary in size and length... designated as an R-group... 20 common amino acids are used as protein building blocks. They differ only in the R-group position.\" Also lines 5115–5116: \"The twenty common naturally occurring amino acids contain an alpha-carbon, an amino, a carboxylic acid, and an R group (or side chain).\" Cross-ref OpenStax Biology 2e, lines 4234–4238: \"the alpha (α) carbon, bonded to an amino group (NH2), a carboxyl group (COOH), and to a hydrogen atom. Every amino acid also has another atom or group of atoms bonded to the central atom known as the R group.\"

**Distractors:**
- **B** — _partial_truth_: Names three of the four real substituents but omits the hydrogen atom. LibreTexts (5104) and OpenStax (4236–4237) both explicitly attach a hydrogen to the alpha-carbon, so the backbone has four groups, not three.  
  ↳ OpenStax Biology 2e, lines 4236–4237: "bonded to an amino group (NH2), a carboxyl group (COOH), and to a hydrogen atom." LibreTexts 5104: "the alpha carbon is also attached to a hydrogen atom."
- **C** — _misconception_: Replaces the conserved carboxyl group with a phosphate group. The backbone acidic group is a carboxylic acid; phosphate appears only when a side-chain -OH is phosphorylated, never as a substituent on the backbone alpha-carbon.  
  ↳ LibreTexts 5115–5116: amino acids "contain an alpha-carbon, an amino, a carboxylic acid, and an R group"; phosphorylation of side-chain -OH groups is described separately (LibreTexts line 3232: "Phosphorylation of -OH containing side chains of proteins by ATP").
- **D** — _misconception_: Counts two R-groups, but only one variable side chain is attached to the alpha-carbon; the remaining three positions are the fixed amino, carboxyl, and hydrogen groups. The amino acids differ only at this single R-group position.  
  ↳ LibreTexts 5105–5106: amino acids "differ only in the R-group position"; the alpha-carbon bears one amino, one carboxyl, one hydrogen, and one R-group.

**Explanation:** This is a Biochemistry question that falls under the content category 'Structure and Function of Proteins and Their Constituent Amino Acids.' The answer is A because every standard amino acid is built on one shared backbone: a central \(\alpha\)-carbon bonded to an amino group, a carboxyl group, a single hydrogen atom, and a variable R-group (side chain). LibreTexts states that \"the alpha carbon is also attached to a hydrogen atom and to one additional group\" and that the 20 common amino acids \"differ only in the R-group position\" (5101–5106), and OpenStax describes the \(\alpha\)-carbon as \"bonded to an amino group, a carboxyl group, and to a hydrogen atom\" plus \"another atom or group of atoms... known as the R group\" (4234–4238). Because the first three groups are constant, it is the fourth substituent — the R-group — that distinguishes one amino acid from another. (Choice A) is correct: it lists exactly the four groups the textbook attaches to the \(\alpha\)-carbon — amino, carboxyl, hydrogen, and R-group. (Choice B) drops the hydrogen atom, but the texts explicitly attach a hydrogen to the \(\alpha\)-carbon, giving four substituents, not three. (Choice C) swaps the carboxyl group for a phosphate group; the conserved acidic group on the backbone is a carboxylic acid, and phosphate appears only when a side-chain \(-\text{OH}\) is later phosphorylated, never on the backbone \(\alpha\)-carbon. (Choice D) duplicates the R-group, but only one variable side chain is attached; the other three positions are the fixed amino, carboxyl, and hydrogen groups. This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall the general structure of an \(\alpha\)-amino acid and the four groups bonded to its \(\alpha\)-carbon.

## Q2 — Amino Acid Structure & Stereochemistry · **easy** · Skill 1 · 75s · conf 5
**Stem:** A researcher completely hydrolyzes a globular protein purified from human liver tissue and determines the absolute configuration of each released \(\alpha\)-amino acid. Excluding glycine, which is achiral, the analysis is most likely to show that every chiral residue has which configuration?

- **A.** The D-configuration
- **B.** The L-configuration ✅
- **C.** A racemic D/L mixture
- **D.** A meso configuration

**Correct (B):** LibreTexts Biochemistry 5407-5408: "All naturally occurring proteins from all living organisms consist of L-amino acids based on their structural similarities to L-glyceraldehyde." LibreTexts 5421: "All naturally occurring amino acids in proteins are L, which corresponds to the S isomer, except cysteine." OpenStax Biology 2e 3318: "In nature, the L-forms of amino acids are predominant in proteins."

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _misconception_: The D-configuration is the unnatural enantiomer; D-amino acids occur in some bacterial cell walls but not in normal proteins, so a human liver protein would not yield D-residues.  
  ↳ OpenStax Biology 2e 3319: "Some D forms of amino acids are seen in the cell walls of bacteria and polypeptides in other organisms." / 26219-26220: "unusual peptides containing both L- and D-amino acids including D-glutamic acid and D-alanine. (Proteins normally have only L-amino acids...)"
- **C** — _partial_truth_: A racemic mixture contains equal amounts of both enantiomers, but proteins are built from a single enantiomer (L), so hydrolysis releases only L-amino acids, not a D/L mixture.  
  ↳ LibreTexts Biochemistry 5407-5408: "All naturally occurring proteins from all living organisms consist of L-amino acids..."
- **D** — _adjacent_fact_: A meso form requires a single molecule with multiple stereocenters and an internal plane of symmetry; a standard alpha-amino acid has only one stereocenter and cannot be meso.  
  ↳ LibreTexts Biochemistry 5421-5424: absolute configuration of the amino acids is shown about the single alpha-carbon (H, COOH, R group, and NH3 via the 'CORN' mnemonic), indicating one stereocenter.

**Explanation:** This is a Biochemistry question that falls under the content category 'Structure and Function of Proteins and Their Constituent Amino Acids.' The answer is B because every chiral amino acid found in naturally occurring proteins is in the L-configuration, related to L-glyceraldehyde. LibreTexts states that "All naturally occurring proteins from all living organisms consist of L-amino acids based on their structural similarities to L-glyceraldehyde" (5407-5408) and that "All naturally occurring amino acids in proteins are L, which corresponds to the S isomer, except cysteine" (5421); OpenStax adds that "In nature, the L-forms of amino acids are predominant in proteins" (3318). Because a protein is built entirely from one enantiomer, hydrolyzing it releases L-amino acids, not a mixture of stereoisomers. (Choice A) The D-configuration is the unnatural enantiomer; OpenStax notes that "Some D forms of amino acids are seen in the cell walls of bacteria" (3319) but not in normal proteins, so this would be wrong for a human liver protein. (Choice B) is correct: the residues hydrolyzed from a natural protein are L-amino acids. (Choice C) A racemic D/L mixture would require equal amounts of both enantiomers, but proteins are made from a single enantiomer (L), so no D-residues are present to create a mixture (5407-5408). (Choice D) A meso configuration requires a single molecule with multiple stereocenters and an internal mirror plane; a standard \(\alpha\)-amino acid has only one stereocenter and cannot be meso (5421). This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall that proteinogenic amino acids in proteins adopt the L-configuration.

## Q3 — Amino Acid Structure & Stereochemistry · **medium** · Skill 2 · 105s · conf 5
**Stem:** A pre-med student examines the \(\alpha\)-carbon of alanine and notes that it is a chiral center, which is why alanine is optically active. The student is asked to identify the structural feature that makes this \(\alpha\)-carbon chiral. Which feature correctly accounts for the chirality of the \(\alpha\)-carbon?

- **A.** It bears a carbon-carbon double bond
- **B.** It has three different groups attached
- **C.** It has four different groups attached ✅
- **D.** Its side chain is always nonpolar

**Correct (C):** OpenStax Biology 2e, line 4244: "Amino acids have a central asymmetric carbon to which an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen atom, and a side chain (R group) are attached." OpenStax Biology 2e, lines 3290-3291: "To be enantiomers, a molecule must have at least four different atoms or groups connected to a central carbon." LibreTexts Biochemistry, line 5399: "The amino acids are all chiral except glycine, whose side chain is H."

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _misconception_: A carbon-carbon double bond gives rise to geometric (cis-trans) isomers, not chirality at a tetrahedral center. OpenStax (lines 3281-3282) describes geometric isomers as having 'a different arrangement of atoms around a double bond,' a category distinct from enantiomers/chirality. The alpha-carbon is sp3 and chiral because of four different substituents, not because of any double bond.  
  ↳ OpenStax Biology 2e, lines 3281-3282: 'Geometric isomers have a different arrangement of atoms around a double bond. (c) Enantiomers are mirror images of each other.' Also line 3287: 'Molecules must have a double bond to be cis-trans isomers.'
- **B** — _partial_truth_: Three different groups is the FALSE threshold: if only three of the four substituents differ, two must be identical, so the carbon is superimposable on its mirror image and is achiral. OpenStax explicitly lists the 'at least three different atoms or groups' statement (option c) as the false statement, and names 'at least four' (option d) as the true requirement for enantiomers.  
  ↳ OpenStax Biology 2e, lines 3288-3289: 'To be enantiomers, a molecule must have at least three different atoms or groups connected to a central carbon' — given as the FALSE statement; contrasted with lines 3290-3291 (four different = true).
- **D** — _adjacent_fact_: Chirality depends on having four DIFFERENT groups, not on the polarity of the side chain. The R group is not always nonpolar — glycine's R group is H (and glycine is achiral), while serine, threonine, and cysteine have polar side chains and lysine and arginine are positively charged/basic. Side-chain polarity classifies the amino acid; it does not determine whether the alpha-carbon is a chiral center.  
  ↳ OpenStax Biology 2e, lines 4277-4279: 'glycine has a hydrogen atom as the R group ... serine, threonine, and cysteine are polar and have hydrophilic side chains. The side chains of lysine and arginine are positively charged.' LibreTexts Biochemistry, line 5399: 'The amino acids are all chiral except glycine, whose side chain is H.'

**Explanation:** This is a Biochemistry question that falls under the content category 'Structure and Function of Proteins and Their Constituent Amino Acids.' The answer is C because the \(\alpha\)-carbon of a standard amino acid is bonded to four different groups — an \(-\text{NH}_3^+\) (amino) group, a \(-\text{COO}^-\) (carboxyl) group, a hydrogen atom, and a distinct side chain (R group) — and a carbon carrying four different substituents is an asymmetric (chiral) center. OpenStax describes amino acids as having "a central asymmetric carbon to which an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen atom, and a side chain (R group) are attached," and explicitly states that to be enantiomers "a molecule must have at least four different atoms or groups connected to a central carbon." LibreTexts adds that "the amino acids are all chiral except glycine, whose side chain is H," confirming that when the four attached groups are all different the molecule is chiral and optically active. (Choice A) is incorrect: a carbon-carbon double bond produces geometric (cis-trans) isomers, which OpenStax classifies as a different arrangement of atoms "around a double bond," not the source of chirality at a tetrahedral center. (Choice B) is incorrect: OpenStax names the "at least three different atoms or groups" statement as the false threshold; three different groups means two substituents are identical, so the carbon is superimposable on its mirror image and is achiral. (Choice C) is correct: four different groups on the \(\alpha\)-carbon make it asymmetric, so the molecule is not superimposable on its mirror image and is optically active. (Choice D) is incorrect: the side chain need not be nonpolar — glycine's R group is simply H (making it achiral), while serine, threonine, and cysteine carry polar side chains and lysine and arginine carry charged ones, so "always nonpolar" is false and is not what creates chirality. This is a Scientific Reasoning and Problem Solving question because you must apply the four-different-groups rule for an asymmetric center to explain why the amino acid \(\alpha\)-carbon is chiral.

## Q4 — Amino Acid Structure & Stereochemistry · **medium** · Skill 2 · 105s · conf 5
**Stem:** A researcher resolves a racemic mixture of a standard amino acid into separate enantiomers that rotate plane-polarized light in opposite directions. When she repeats the procedure with one particular proteinogenic amino acid, she CANNOT obtain two distinct enantiomers because its \(\alpha\)-carbon is bonded to only three different groups. Which amino acid is she most likely working with in the second trial?

- **A.** Alanine
- **B.** Proline
- **C.** Serine
- **D.** Glycine ✅

**Correct (D):** LibreTexts Biochemistry, Section 3.1.7 Amino Acid Stereochemistry, line 5399: "The amino acids are all chiral except glycine, whose side chain is H. A chiral molecule is not superimposable with its mirror image." Reinforced at line 5243 ("Glycine, the simplest amino acid, has a single hydrogen atoms as the 'side' chain") and OpenStax Biology 2e line 4277 ("the amino acid glycine has a hydrogen atom as the R group").

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _misconception_: Alanine is the smallest CHIRAL amino acid (its side chain is a methyl group, not a hydrogen), so its alpha-carbon has four different groups and it IS optically active and resolvable. Students who recall 'smallest amino acid' but conflate it with 'achiral' fall for this 510-style trap.  
  ↳ LibreTexts Biochemistry line 5242: alanine listed among the aliphatic amino acids with hydrocarbon side chains; line 5399 states all amino acids are chiral EXCEPT glycine, so alanine is chiral.
- **B** — _adjacent_fact_: Proline is the structurally unusual amino acid (its side chain cyclizes onto the amino nitrogen forming a five-membered ring), but this cyclization does not remove chirality—its alpha-carbon still bears four different groups, so it remains chiral and optically active.  
  ↳ LibreTexts Biochemistry lines 5243-5244: proline's 'hydrocarbon side chain has cyclized with the terminal amine, creating a unique 5-membered ring'; line 5399 confirms only glycine is achiral.
- **C** — _adjacent_fact_: Serine is a polar amino acid with a -CH2OH side chain and four different groups on its alpha-carbon; it is chiral and exists as resolvable L and D enantiomers, so it is optically active.  
  ↳ LibreTexts Biochemistry line 5449 explicitly depicting 'L-Serine' and 'D-Serine'; line 5399 lists only glycine as the achiral exception.

**Explanation:** This is a Biochemistry question that falls under the content category 'Structure and Function of Proteins and Their Constituent Amino Acids.' The answer is D because glycine is the only standard amino acid whose side chain is a single hydrogen atom; its \(\alpha\)-carbon therefore carries two identical hydrogens and only three distinct substituents (\(-\text{NH}_3^+\), \(-\text{COO}^-\), and a hydrogen, with the side-chain H duplicating the existing H), so the \(\alpha\)-carbon is not a stereocenter. The textbook states, "The amino acids are all chiral except glycine, whose side chain is H," and that a chiral molecule "is not superimposable with its mirror image." Because glycine is superimposable on its mirror image, it has no enantiomers and is not optically active, so the resolution described cannot succeed. The other three choices all possess four different groups on the \(\alpha\)-carbon and so exist as resolvable enantiomers. (Choice A) Alanine is the smallest CHIRAL amino acid—its side chain is a methyl group, giving four different substituents on the \(\alpha\)-carbon, so it is optically active and can be resolved. (Choice B) Proline is cyclic (its side chain bonds back to the amino nitrogen to form a five-membered ring), but it still has four distinct groups around the \(\alpha\)-carbon and remains chiral. (Choice C) Serine bears a \(-\text{CH}_2\text{OH}\) side chain and is a polar, chiral amino acid; the textbook explicitly depicts both L-serine and D-serine, confirming it has two enantiomers. (Choice D) is correct: glycine's \(-\text{H}\) side chain leaves only three different groups on the \(\alpha\)-carbon, making it achiral and not optically active. This is a Scientific Reasoning and Problem Solving question because you must apply the rule that four different groups on a carbon are required for chirality to identify the one standard amino acid that is the exception.

**Explanation:** This is a Biochemistry question that falls under the content category 'Structure and Function of Proteins and Their Constituent Amino Acids.' (Choice A) is correct: cysteine's side-chain sulfur raises its priority above the \(\alpha\)-carboxyl carbon, producing the \(R\) label while the residue stays L. (Choice B) Methionine also contains sulfur, but its sulfur sits in a thioether deeper in a nonpolar side chain, not directly at the priority-determining position; methionine remains an \(S\)-configured L-amino acid (LibreTexts 5257-5258). (Choice C) Serine is the oxygen analog of cysteine with a \(-\text{CH}_2\text{OH}\) side chain; oxygen does not outrank the carboxyl carbon in the same way, so L-serine is \(S\) (LibreTexts 5263, 5449). (Choice D) Glycine has a hydrogen side chain, so it is achiral and has no \(R\) or \(S\) descriptor at all (LibreTexts 5399). This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall the structural reason that side-chain sulfur reorders priorities and gives cysteine an \(R\) descriptor within the L family.

## Q7 — Amino Acid Structure & Stereochemistry · **hard** · Skill 2 · 150s · conf 5

  ↳ LibreTexts Biochemistry, line 5421: 'All naturally occurring amino acids in proteins are L, which corresponds to the S isomer, except cysteine'; lines 5460-5461: '...does not reflect a real difference in configuration.'

(Choice B) is correct: the higher priority of sulfur over oxygen/carbon reorders the CIP ranking and switches the label without changing the spatial arrangement (LibreTexts 5460-5461). (Choice C) is incorrect: every amino acid except glycine has a single chiral center, and the sulfhydryl group is a simple \(-\text{SH}\), so cysteine has only one stereocenter, not two (LibreTexts 5399, 5263). (Choice D) is incorrect: serine's \(\alpha\)-carbon bears four different groups and is chiral, since only glycine ("whose side chain is H") is achiral (LibreTexts 5399). This is a Scientific Reasoning and Problem Solving question because you must apply the CIP priority concept to explain why two structurally analogous L-amino acids receive different (R)/(S) labels.

## Q8 — Side-Chain Classification · **easy** · Skill 1 · 70s · conf 5

- **A.** Lysine and arginine
- **B.** Asparagine and glutamine
