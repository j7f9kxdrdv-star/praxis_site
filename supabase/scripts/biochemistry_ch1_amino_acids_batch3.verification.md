## Q1 — Peptide Bond Formation & Hydrolysis · **easy** · Skill 1 · 70s · conf 5
**Stem:** During protein synthesis, a ribosome joins the \(\alpha\)-carboxyl group of one amino acid to the \(\alpha\)-amino group of the next, forming a peptide bond. As this single new bond forms, the reaction releases which of the following molecules?

- **A.** A molecule of ATP
- **B.** A molecule of water ✅
- **C.** A free phosphate group
- **D.** A hydrogen ion only

**Correct (B):** libretexts-biochemistry.txt line 5138-5139: "The resulting link between the amino acids is an amide link, which biochemists call a peptide bond. In this reaction, water is released."; line 6401-6402: "The amino acids are linked together using dehydration synthesis (loss of water) reaction that connects the carboxylic acid of the upstream amino acid with the amine functional group of the downstream amino acid to form an amide linkage." Cross-ref openstax-biology-2e.txt line 4291-4293: "One amino acid's carboxyl group and the incoming amino acid's amino group combine, releasing a water molecule. The resulting bond is the peptide bond."

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _process_step_confusion_: ATP is consumed (hydrolyzed) to activate the carboxyl group before bond formation; it is not the byproduct released when the amide bond forms. LibreTexts line 6406-6407 describes ATP-based activation as a separate prior step, while the bond-forming dehydration releases water (line 5139).  
  ↳ libretexts-biochemistry.txt line 6406-6407: "The activation reaction... involves the transfer of a phosphate from a phosphoanhydride, ATP, to the carboxylic acid group, forming a mixed anhydride with the phosphate serving as a leaving group."
- **C** — _adjacent_fact_: Free phosphate is associated with the upstream ATP activation step (a phosphate leaving group), not with the dehydration synthesis that forms the peptide bond, which releases water. This conflates the activation chemistry with the condensation byproduct.  
  ↳ libretexts-biochemistry.txt line 6404-6407: "...activating the carboxylic acid end to form a derivative with a better leaving group... transfer of a phosphate from... ATP... with the phosphate serving as a leaving group." vs. line 6401-6402 dehydration synthesis (loss of water).
- **D** — _misconception_: The condensation expels a full \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\) molecule (an -OH from the carboxyl and an -H from the amine), not merely a lone hydrogen ion; treating the byproduct as just a proton ignores the -OH contributed by the carboxyl group.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt line 4291-4293: "One amino acid's carboxyl group and the incoming amino acid's amino group combine, releasing a water molecule."

**Explanation:** This is a Biochemistry question that falls under the content category 'Structure and Function of Proteins and Their Constituent Amino Acids.' The answer is B because a peptide bond is an amide linkage formed by dehydration synthesis (condensation), in which the \(\alpha\)-carboxyl of one amino acid joins the \(\alpha\)-amino of the next and one molecule of \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\) is released. LibreTexts states the amino group attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon and "In this reaction, water is released," and that "The amino acids are linked together using dehydration synthesis (loss of water) reaction." OpenStax likewise notes the two groups "combine, releasing a water molecule." The reverse reaction, hydrolysis, consumes water to break the bond. (Choice A) is incorrect: ATP is hydrolyzed to activate the carboxyl group before the bond forms, but it is not the molecule released as the amide bond itself is created. (Choice B) is correct: forming the amide (peptide) bond is a condensation that expels exactly one molecule of water per bond. (Choice C) is incorrect: the activation step uses ATP and produces a phosphate-containing intermediate, but the dehydration synthesis that makes the peptide bond releases water, not free phosphate. (Choice D) is incorrect: the released water can be viewed as -OH from the carboxyl plus -H from the amine, but the net byproduct is a full water molecule, not a lone proton. This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall that peptide bond formation is a dehydration (condensation) reaction that releases water.

## Q2 — Peptide Bond Formation & Hydrolysis · **easy** · Skill 2 · 70s · conf 5
**Stem:** A biochemist characterizes a newly isolated, unbranched single-chain peptide and determines that it is composed of exactly seven amino acid residues joined end to end. Based on this information, how many peptide bonds most likely link the residues of this linear heptapeptide?

- **A.** Six peptide bonds ✅
- **B.** Seven peptide bonds
- **C.** Five peptide bonds
- **D.** Eight peptide bonds

**Correct (A):** LibreTexts Biochemistry, libretexts-biochemistry.txt line 5155: "The resulting structure is called a dipeptide when an amide bond links two amino acids. Likewise, we can have tripeptides, tetrapeptides, and other polypeptides." Cross-ref OpenStax Biology 2e, openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 18568-18569: "A peptide bond links the carboxyl end of one amino acid with the amino end of another, producing one water molecule during the process." Together these establish one amide bond per adjacent residue pair, so a linear N-residue chain has N-1 peptide bonds (here 7-1=6).

**Distractors:**
- **B** — _scale_unit_error / off_by_one_counted_residues_not_bonds_: Seven equals the residue count, not the bond count. A chain of N residues has only N-1 internal peptide bonds because the N-terminal and C-terminal residues each present a free (unbonded) terminus. LibreTexts shows that two amino acids ('a dipeptide') are linked by a single amide bond, so residues always exceed bonds by one in a linear peptide.  
  ↳ libretexts-biochemistry.txt line 5155: "The resulting structure is called a dipeptide when an amide bond links two amino acids."
- **C** — _scale_unit_error / off_by_one_under_count_N_minus_2_: Five is N-2, which under-counts the bonds by one. The N-1 rule gives 7-1=6 for a heptapeptide; a value of five would instead correspond to a six-residue chain (hexapeptide). Each adjacent pair of residues contributes exactly one amide link, and a seven-residue chain has six adjacent pairs.  
  ↳ libretexts-biochemistry.txt line 5155: "The resulting structure is called a dipeptide when an amide bond links two amino acids. Likewise, we can have tripeptides, tetrapeptides, and other polypeptides."
- **D** — _scale_unit_error / off_by_one_over_count_N_plus_1_: Eight is N+1, which over-counts the bonds. An unbranched, single-chain peptide of N residues cannot exceed N-1 peptide bonds; more bonds would require branching or cyclization, but the chain is explicitly unbranched and single-chain. OpenStax confirms one water (one bond) is produced per adjacent pair, so seven residues yield six bonds.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 18568-18569: "A peptide bond links the carboxyl end of one amino acid with the amino end of another, producing one water molecule during the process."

**Explanation:** This is a Biochemistry question that falls under the content category 'Structure and Function of Proteins and Their Constituent Amino Acids.' The answer is A because each peptide (amide) bond joins one adjacent pair of residues, so a linear chain of \(N\) residues has \(N-1\) such links. LibreTexts states that "the resulting structure is called a dipeptide when an amide bond links two amino acids," i.e., two residues are joined by one bond; adding each additional residue creates exactly one more amide link (LibreTexts 3.1). Because forming the bond is a dehydration reaction, OpenStax notes that the bond links "the carboxyl end of one amino acid with the amino end of another, producing one water molecule during the process," confirming one bond per adjacent pair. For an unbranched heptapeptide, \(N=7\), so the number of peptide bonds is \(7-1=6\). (Choice A) is correct: a linear seven-residue chain has six junctions between adjacent residues, and each junction is one peptide bond, giving \(7-1=6\). (Choice B) is wrong: seven counts the residues themselves, not the bonds; a chain of \(N\) residues has only \(N-1\) internal links because the two end residues each contribute a free terminus rather than a bond. (Choice C) is wrong: five would be \(N-2\), which under-counts by one and would describe a six-residue (hexapeptide) chain, not a heptapeptide. (Choice D) is wrong: eight is \(N+1\); a linear peptide cannot have more bonds than \(N-1\) unless it were branched or cyclic, and the chain here is explicitly unbranched and single-chain. This is a Scientific Reasoning and Problem Solving question because you must apply the \(N-1\) relationship between residue count and peptide-bond count to a specific linear chain length.

## Q3 — Peptide Bond Formation & Hydrolysis · **medium** · Skill 2 · 105s · conf 5
**Stem:** A protease cleaves the single peptide bond of the dipeptide Ser-Val, releasing free serine and free valine. As this amide linkage is broken, how does the reaction account for water relative to the dehydration synthesis that originally formed the bond?

- **A.** One molecule of water is released
- **B.** Two molecules of water are consumed
- **C.** One molecule of water is consumed ✅
- **D.** No net water is gained or lost

**Correct (C):** libretexts-biochemistry.txt lines 6407-6410: "Note that the reverse reaction, hydrolysis, requires the incorporation of a water molecule to separate two amino acids and break the amide bond... a class of enzymes called proteases catalyzes protein hydrolysis." Also line 5139: "In this reaction, water is released. In a reverse reaction, the peptide bond can be cleaved by water (hydrolysis)."

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _reversed_relationship_: Releasing one water describes the dehydration synthesis that FORMS the peptide bond, not the hydrolysis that breaks it. The textbook (line 5139) says 'In this reaction [bond formation], water is released. In a reverse reaction, the peptide bond can be cleaved by water (hydrolysis),' so cleavage consumes water rather than releasing it.  
  ↳ libretexts-biochemistry.txt line 5139: "In this reaction, water is released. In a reverse reaction, the peptide bond can be cleaved by water (hydrolysis)."
- **B** — _scale_unit_error_: A dipeptide contains exactly one peptide bond, so only one water can be incorporated. The textbook (line 6408) specifies hydrolysis 'requires the incorporation of a water molecule' (singular) to break the amide bond; two waters would imply two bonds were broken.  
  ↳ libretexts-biochemistry.txt line 6408: "the reverse reaction, hydrolysis, requires the incorporation of a water molecule to separate two amino acids and break the amide bond."
- **D** — _misconception_: Treating cleavage as water-neutral ignores that the bond-breaking step IS hydrolysis, which by definition incorporates water. The textbook (line 6408) states hydrolysis 'requires the incorporation of a water molecule,' so there is a net consumption of one water, not zero.  
  ↳ libretexts-biochemistry.txt line 6408: "the reverse reaction, hydrolysis, requires the incorporation of a water molecule to separate two amino acids and break the amide bond."

**Explanation:** This is a Biochemistry question that falls under the content category 'Structure and Function of Proteins and Their Constituent Amino Acids.' The answer is C because hydrolysis is the exact reverse of the dehydration synthesis (condensation) that built the peptide bond: where bond formation released one \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\), cleaving that one amide bond incorporates one \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\) to separate the two residues. The textbook states that amino acids are linked by a 'dehydration synthesis (loss of water) reaction' and that 'the reverse reaction, hydrolysis, requires the incorporation of a water molecule to separate two amino acids and break the amide bond.' Because Ser-Val contains exactly one peptide bond, exactly one water is consumed to regenerate the free \(-\text{COO}^-\) and \(-\text{NH}_3^+\) termini. (Choice A) describes condensation, not hydrolysis; releasing water is what happens when the bond forms, so it reverses the actual direction of cleavage. (Choice B) overcounts: a dipeptide has only one peptide bond, so only one water can be added, not two. (Choice C) is correct: breaking the single amide bond by hydrolysis consumes one molecule of water, the mirror image of the single water lost during synthesis. (Choice D) ignores that the bond-breaking step is itself a hydrolysis, which by definition incorporates a water molecule rather than balancing out to zero. This is a Scientific Reasoning and Problem Solving question because you must apply the principle that hydrolysis reverses condensation to determine the direction and stoichiometry of water for a specific dipeptide.

## Q4 — Peptide Bond Formation & Hydrolysis · **hard** · Skill 2 · 150s · conf 5
**Stem:** A ribosome joins valine and serine to form the dipeptide Val-Ser, releasing one molecule of \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\). For the new amide linkage to form, which atom donates the electron pair that initiates the reaction, and which atom receives that attack?

- **A.** The serine carbonyl oxygen attacks the valine \(\alpha\)-amino nitrogen
- **B.** The valine carbonyl carbon attacks the serine \(\alpha\)-amino nitrogen
- **C.** The serine \(\alpha\)-amino nitrogen attacks the serine carbonyl carbon
- **D.** The serine \(\alpha\)-amino nitrogen attacks the valine carbonyl carbon ✅

**Correct (D):** libretexts-biochemistry.txt lines 5136-5139: "Amino acids form polymers through a nucleophilic attack by the amino group of an amino acid at the electrophilic carbonyl carbon of the carboxyl group of another amino acid. ... In this reaction, water is released." And lines 6404-6405: "This carbonyl of the modified end serves as an electrophile in the attack of the amine nitrogen, a nucleophile, in a nucleophilic substitution reaction." Directionality lines 6411-6413: "Subsequent amino acids will be added to the carboxylic acid terminal of the growing structure. Proteins are always synthesized directionally, starting with the amino terminus and ending with the carboxy terminus."

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _misconception_: Names the carbonyl oxygen, not the amine nitrogen, as the attacking species and makes the nitrogen the target. The textbook identifies the amine nitrogen as the nucleophile and the carbonyl carbon (not oxygen) as the electrophile (lines 6404-6405: 'This carbonyl ... serves as an electrophile in the attack of the amine nitrogen, a nucleophile'). The carbonyl oxygen is not the nucleophile in amide formation.  
  ↳ libretexts-biochemistry.txt lines 6404-6405: 'This carbonyl of the modified end serves as an electrophile in the attack of the amine nitrogen, a nucleophile, in a nucleophilic substitution reaction.'
- **B** — _reversed_relationship_: Identifies the correct two centers (valine carbonyl carbon and serine amino nitrogen) but reverses their roles, casting the electrophilic carbon as the attacker and the nucleophilic nitrogen as the target. The textbook is explicit that the amino group performs the nucleophilic attack at the electrophilic carbonyl carbon, not the reverse.  
  ↳ libretexts-biochemistry.txt lines 5136-5138: 'Amino acids form polymers through a nucleophilic attack by the amino group of an amino acid at the electrophilic carbonyl carbon of the carboxyl group of another amino acid.'
- **C** — _process_step_confusion_: Keeps the correct nucleophile (an amine nitrogen) but directs it at serine's own carbonyl carbon, which is serine's C-terminal carboxyl. Because the peptide is written N-to-C as Val-Ser, valine supplies the carboxyl/carbonyl that is attacked; an intramolecular attack on serine's own carboxyl would not link the two residues and ignores N-to-C directionality.  
  ↳ libretexts-biochemistry.txt lines 6411-6413: 'Subsequent amino acids will be added to the carboxylic acid terminal of the growing structure. Proteins are always synthesized directionally, starting with the amino terminus and ending with the carboxy terminus.'

**Explanation:** This is a Biochemistry question that falls under the content category 'Structure and Function of Proteins and Their Constituent Amino Acids.' The answer is D because in peptide-bond formation the amino group of one residue performs a nucleophilic attack at the electrophilic carbonyl carbon of the activated carboxyl group of the other residue. The textbook states that "Amino acids form polymers through a nucleophilic attack by the amino group of an amino acid at the electrophilic carbonyl carbon of the carboxyl group of another amino acid" and that "This carbonyl of the modified end serves as an electrophile in the attack of the amine nitrogen, a nucleophile." Because the dipeptide is written N-to-C as Val-Ser, valine is the upstream (N-terminal) residue contributing its activated \(\alpha\)-carboxyl carbonyl carbon, and serine is the downstream residue contributing its \(\alpha\)-amino nitrogen; the serine nitrogen (nucleophile, electron-pair donor) therefore attacks the valine carbonyl carbon (electrophile), releasing one \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\). (Choice A) reverses the roles: oxygen is not the attacking species here, and pairing it with the amino nitrogen as the target inverts both the nucleophile and the electrophile. (Choice B) correctly pairs valine's carbonyl with serine's nitrogen but reverses which is nucleophile and which is electrophile—it names the electrophilic carbon as the attacker. (Choice C) keeps the correct nucleophile (the amine nitrogen) but attacks the wrong carbonyl: serine's own carbonyl is the C-terminal carboxyl, so this would not link valine to serine and contradicts the N-to-C order. (Choice D) is correct: the serine \(\alpha\)-amino nitrogen is the nucleophile and the valine carbonyl carbon is the electrophile. This is a Scientific Reasoning and Problem Solving question because you must apply the peptide-bond mechanism to a specific named sequence, using N-to-C directionality to assign which residue supplies the nucleophile and which supplies the electrophile.
