## Q1 — Tertiary Structure & Folding · **easy** · Skill 1 · 75s · conf 5
**Stem:** A globular enzyme is stabilized by several side-chain interactions that hold its single polypeptide in a compact native fold. One of these interactions is the only covalent crosslink contributing to the protein's tertiary structure. This crosslink is formed by which of the following?

- **A.** Oxidation of two cysteine thiols ✅
- **B.** Clustering of nonpolar side chains
- **C.** Attraction between oppositely charged side chains
- **D.** Hydrogen bonding along the backbone

**Correct (A):** libretexts-biochemistry.txt lines 5892-5894: "Two cysteine side chains can covalently interact in a protein to form a disulfide bond (RS-SR), called cystine. ... RSSR is the oxidized form (S oxidation number -1), and RSH is the reduced form (S oxidation number -2) of thiols." and line 6613: "Disulfide bonds within individual chains and between them stabilize both tertiary and quaternary structures." Cross-ref openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 4437-4438: "Interaction between cysteine side chains forms disulfide linkages in the presence of oxygen, the only covalent bond that forms during protein folding."

**Distractors:**
- **B** — _adjacent_fact_: Clustering of nonpolar side chains is the hydrophobic interaction that drives folding, but it is noncovalent (induced dipole-induced dipole), not the covalent crosslink the stem asks for. LibreTexts: 'induced dipole-induced dipole interactions are often called hydrophobic interactions' (line 11101).  
  ↳ libretexts-biochemistry.txt line 11101: 'Ion-ion interactions are usually called salt bridges, and induced dipole-induced dipole interactions are often called hydrophobic interactions.'
- **C** — _adjacent_fact_: Attraction between oppositely charged side chains is a salt bridge, an ion-ion electrostatic interaction that stabilizes tertiary structure noncovalently; it is not a covalent bond. LibreTexts: 'we ... often give it a different name, a salt bridge, as the ion-ion interaction bridges the distal parts of the larger molecule. We also categorize it as an ion-ion noncovalent attraction' (line 3938-3939).  
  ↳ libretexts-biochemistry.txt lines 3938-3939: 'we ... often give it a different name, a salt bridge, as the ion-ion interaction bridges the distal parts of the larger molecule. We also categorize it as an ion-ion noncovalent attraction.' and line 11100-11101: 'Ion-ion interactions are usually called salt bridges.'
- **D** — _process_step_confusion_: Hydrogen bonding along the backbone defines secondary structure (alpha-helix/beta-sheet) and is noncovalent, so it is neither the tertiary level nor the covalent crosslink described. LibreTexts: secondary structure arises 'when hydrogen bonds between the peptide backbone amide hydrogens and carbonyl oxygens occur' (line 6610-6611).  
  ↳ libretexts-biochemistry.txt lines 6610-6611: 'secondary structure: regular repeating structures arising when hydrogen bonds between the peptide backbone amide hydrogens and carbonyl oxygens occur at regular intervals.'

**Explanation:** This is a Biochemistry question that falls under the content category 'Structure and Function of Proteins and Their Constituent Amino Acids.' The answer is A because a disulfide bond is a covalent \(-\text{S}-\text{S}-\) crosslink formed when two cysteine side-chain thiols are oxidized to give cystine. LibreTexts states that "Two cysteine side chains can covalently interact in a protein to form a disulfide bond (RS-SR), called cystine," with "RSSR ... the oxidized form (S oxidation number -1)" relative to the reduced thiol (line 5892-5894), and that "Disulfide bonds within individual chains and between them stabilize both tertiary and quaternary structures" (line 6613). OpenStax adds that the "Interaction between cysteine side chains forms disulfide linkages in the presence of oxygen, the only covalent bond that forms during protein folding" (line 4437-4438). (Choice A) is correct: it names the oxidation of two cysteine thiols, the reaction that produces the covalent disulfide crosslink. (Choice B) is wrong because clustering of nonpolar side chains is a noncovalent hydrophobic interaction ("induced dipole-induced dipole interactions are often called hydrophobic interactions," line 11101), not a covalent crosslink. (Choice C) is wrong because attraction between oppositely charged side chains is a salt bridge, an "ion-ion" noncovalent interaction (line 11100), not a covalent bond. (Choice D) is wrong because backbone hydrogen bonding defines secondary structure (it produces "regular repeating structures arising when hydrogen bonds between the peptide backbone amide hydrogens and carbonyl oxygens occur," line 6610-6611) and is noncovalent, not the covalent tertiary crosslink described. This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall that disulfide bonds are covalent crosslinks formed by oxidation of cysteine thiols.

## Q3 — Tertiary Structure & Folding · **medium** · Skill 2 · 105s · conf 5
**Stem:** As a globular protein folds, a nonpolar leucine side chain moves from the aqueous surface into the protein's tightly packed interior. This burial is a major driving force for folding. Which change in the surrounding solvent makes this step thermodynamically favorable?

- **A.** Ordered water cages form, lowering solvent entropy
- **B.** New backbone hydrogen bonds lower the system enthalpy
- **C.** Ordered cage water is released, raising solvent entropy ✅
- **D.** Cysteine thiols oxidize, forming a covalent crosslink

**Correct (C):** libretexts-biochemistry.txt, lines 4749-4752: "In the reverse process, transferring the hydrocarbon from water to the pure liquid dissipates the cavity, thereby increasing the number of available microstates for the released solvent, bulk water. This entropic contribution favors the movement of a hydrocarbon from water to the pure hydrocarbon lipid." Reinforced lines 14714-14716 (folding free-energy diagram: hydrophobic effect = -TΔS << 0) and line 14692 (hydrophobic effect among the largest contributors to native-state stabilization).

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _reversed_relationship_: This describes the disfavored direction with the entropy sign reversed. Forming ordered water cages around an exposed nonpolar group LOWERS solvent entropy (the entropic penalty), so it cannot be what makes burial favorable; burial does the opposite — it releases cage water.  
  ↳ libretexts-biochemistry.txt, lines 4746-4748: "A literal cavity must be created to accommodate a hydrocarbon in water. The creation of this more ordered cavity must be entropically disfavored (again because the process proceeds to a state with fewer microstates and lower positional entropy)."
- **B** — _partial_truth_: Backbone hydrogen bonds do contribute to folding via enthalpy (ΔH << 0), but they define SECONDARY structure, not the solvent change asked about, and the favorable solvent term for nonpolar burial is the entropy-driven hydrophobic effect (-TΔS << 0). Names the wrong driving force and the wrong structural level.  
  ↳ libretexts-biochemistry.txt, lines 14714-14722 (folding free-energy diagram: hydrophobic effect -TΔS << 0; H bonds ΔH << 0 as a separate contribution); lines 11243-11244: "The secondary structure is the repetitive structure formed from H-bonds among backbone amide H and carbonyl O atoms."
- **D** — _adjacent_fact_: Disulfide bond formation by oxidation of two cysteine thiols is a real tertiary-structure stabilizing interaction, but it is a covalent crosslink within/between chains — it does not involve the surrounding solvent or the entropy change that makes hydrophobic burial favorable.  
  ↳ libretexts-biochemistry.txt, lines 5892-5894: "Two cysteine side chains can covalently interact in a protein to form a disulfide bond (RS-SR), called cystine ... RSSR is the oxidized form (S oxidation number -1), and RSH is the reduced form (S oxidation number -2) of thiols."

**Explanation:** This is a Biochemistry question that falls under the content category 'Structure and Function of Proteins and Their Constituent Amino Acids.' The answer is C because burying a nonpolar side chain releases the ordered, 'ice-like' water cage that had surrounded it when it was solvent-exposed, and the textbook states that this dissipation of the cavity increases the number of microstates available to the released bulk water, an entropy gain that makes the process spontaneous: "transferring the hydrocarbon from water to the pure liquid dissipates the cavity, thereby increasing the number of available microstates for the released solvent, bulk water" (libretexts-biochemistry.txt, lines 4749-4752). The hydrophobic effect is represented in the folding free-energy diagram as the favorable entropy term \(-T\Delta S \ll 0\) and is named a largest contributor to native-state stabilization (libretexts-biochemistry.txt, lines 14714-14716, 14692). (Choice A) reverses the sign of the entropy change: forming ordered water cages around an exposed nonpolar group lowers solvent entropy and is the unfavorable, disfavored direction, not the one that drives burial (libretexts-biochemistry.txt, lines 4746-4748). (Choice B) is a partial truth that names the wrong driving force and the wrong structural level — backbone hydrogen bonds belong to secondary structure and contribute through enthalpy \(\Delta H \ll 0\), but the question asks about the solvent change that makes hydrophobic burial favorable, which is the entropic term \(-T\Delta S \ll 0\) (libretexts-biochemistry.txt, lines 14718-14719, 11243-11244). (Choice C) is correct: releasing the structured cage water raises solvent entropy and drives folding (libretexts-biochemistry.txt, lines 4749-4752). (Choice D) describes disulfide bond formation, a separate covalent tertiary-structure interaction formed by oxidizing two cysteine thiols, which does not involve the solvent-entropy change that makes nonpolar burial favorable (libretexts-biochemistry.txt, lines 5892-5894). This is a Scientific Reasoning and Problem Solving question because you must apply the thermodynamics of the hydrophobic effect to predict the sign of the solvent entropy change for burying a nonpolar residue.

- **B.** A salt bridge between charged side chains

  ↳ LibreTexts Biochemistry lines 6610–6611: 'secondary structure: regular repeating structures arising when hydrogen bonds between the peptide backbone amide hydrogens and carbonyl oxygens occur at regular intervals.'

  ↳ LibreTexts Biochemistry lines 3938–3940: 'a different name, a salt bridge, as the ion-ion interaction bridges the distal parts of the larger molecule ... a salt bridge/ion-ion interaction ... between the side chains of two amino acids.'

The textbook states that "two cysteine side chains can covalently interact in a protein to form a disulfide bond (RS-SR), called cystine," where "RSSR is the oxidized form ... and RSH is the reduced form" (LibreTexts 5892–5894), and that disulfides "can be cleaved by reducing agents such as beta-mercaptoethanol, dithiothreitol, or tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP)" (LibreTexts 5953–5955). (Choice B) is wrong because a salt bridge is a noncovalent ion-ion interaction between charged side chains (LibreTexts 3938–3940) and is not the target of a thiol-reducing agent. - **B.** Solvent \(\Delta S>0\); exposure \(\Delta G<0\)

The textbook states that "a literal cavity must be created to accommodate a hydrocarbon in water. (Choice C) keeps the correct unfavorable \(\Delta G>0\) but wrongly claims solvent \(\Delta S>0\); exposing the nonpolar group orders the water rather than disordering it, so \(\Delta S<0\) (lines 4746-4748). (Choice D) correctly takes \(\Delta S<0\) but then claims \(\Delta G<0\); a negative solvent entropy change makes \(-T\Delta S\) positive, so by \(\Delta G=\Delta H-T\Delta S\) the entropy-dominated exposure step is unfavorable, \(\Delta G>0\), not spontaneous (lines 4747-4748, 4827).
