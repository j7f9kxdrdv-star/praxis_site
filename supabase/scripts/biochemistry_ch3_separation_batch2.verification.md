### Q1 — Electrophoresis (medium, Skill 2)

The steady-state migration velocity of each protein is described by \(v = Ez/f\), where \(E\) is the field strength, \(z\) is the net charge, and \(f\) is the frictional coefficient. After the field is applied, how will the two proteins migrate relative to each other?  
**Answer:** A · **Foundation:** 5C · **Confidence:** 5  
**Answer citation:** Line 7437: "Hence v/E = Q/f = U = the electrophoretic mobility"; Line 7442: "the electrophoretic mobility U is proportional to the particle's charge density (Q/Rs), not just its size"; Lines 7476-7477: "imagine two globular proteins of different sizes but with compensatory charge differences, which might allow them to migrate at the same speed through the gel."  
**Distractor citation 1:** Line 7421-7422 (for B, size/friction term ignored): "The frictional coefficient depends on a molecule's size and shape. The larger the molecule, the larger the frictional coefficient (i.e., more resistance to the molecule's motion)."  
**Distractor citation 2:** Line 7442 (for C, charge term ignored): "the electrophoretic mobility U is proportional to the particle's charge density (Q/Rs), not just its size"  
**Distractor citation 3:** Lines 7478-7479 (for D, pI relevance): "Some proteins are negatively charged (pH > pI), some would be neutral (pH=pI), and the rest would be positive (pH < pI)" — pI governs the sign/magnitude of net charge, which is already specified here.

### Q3 — Electrophoresis (medium, Skill 2)
**Stem:** A researcher runs two small nucleic-acid fragments, P and Q, in the same electrophoresis apparatus so that both experience an identical applied electric field. Fragment P carries a net charge of \(-4\) and has a frictional coefficient of \(2.0\) (arbitrary units). Fragment Q carries a net charge of \(-6\) and, because it is more compact, has a frictional coefficient of \(1.5\) (same units). Both fragments migrate toward the anode. Once each fragment reaches a steady migration velocity, how do their velocities compare, and why?  
**Answer:** A · **Foundation:** 5C · **Confidence:** 5  
**Answer citation:** Line 7437: 'Hence v/E = Q/f = U = the electrophoretic mobility' — so v = QE/f, meaning velocity rises with charge Q and falls with frictional coefficient f, so the fragment with greater charge and smaller f (Q) migrates faster.  
**Distractor citation 1:** Line 7419-7422 (Choice B): 'v is the particle's velocity, and f is a constant called the frictional coefficient... The frictional coefficient depends on a molecule's size and shape' — friction depends on size/shape, not charge, so greater charge does not increase the frictional (retarding) force.  
**Distractor citation 2:** Line 7395-7396 & 7414-7419 (Choice C): 'FE, the force exerted on the charged particle by the field' and 'Ff, the frictional force... v is the particle's velocity, and f is a constant called the frictional coefficient' — f appears only in the retarding frictional force Ff = fv, not in the driving force FE, so a smaller f does not reduce the driving force.  
**Distractor citation 3:** Line 7442-7443 (Choice D): 'the electrophoretic mobility U is proportional to the particle's charge density (Q/Rs)... Macromolecules of different charge densities can thus be separated by electrophoresis' — molecules in the same field separate by differing charge density, so an identical field does not produce identical velocities.

### Q9 — SDS-PAGE (medium, Skill 2)
**Stem:** A researcher wants to estimate the molecular mass of a newly isolated globular protein, X. She first runs a native (nondenaturing) polyacrylamide gel loaded with X alongside two mass standards of known molecular weight. On this gel, protein X migrates farther than a standard that is actually lighter than X and less far than a standard that is actually heavier than X, so its position relative to the standards does not track molecular weight. Which change to her procedure would allow her to reliably read protein X's molecular mass off a plot of the standards?  
**Answer:** A · **Foundation:** 5C · **Confidence:** 5  
**Answer citation:** Lines 7481-7491: 'run the gel under denaturing conditions so that all proteins have the same charge density. The denaturant of choice ... is usually sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) ... about 1.4 g SDS binding per gram of protein ... the binding of SDS masks any of the charges on the protein and gives all proteins an overall large negative charge ... Since the amount of SDS bound per unit protein mass is constant, the overall charge density on all proteins is similar, so the electrophoretic mobility is only determined by sieving effects ... Mobility becomes only a function of the molecular weight of the protein and not its shape. The molecular weight of an unknown protein can be determined by comparing the protein's position on an SDS polyacrylamide gel with a series of known molecular weight standards.' Also Line 7982: 'SDS-PAGE: By binding to proteins and imparting a uniform negative charge, SDS standardizes the electrophoretic mobility of proteins so that separation is primarily based on molecular weight.'  
**Distractor citation 1:** Choice B (misconception): Line 7442: 'the electrophoretic mobility U is proportional to the particle's charge density (Q/Rs), not just its size' — voltage sets field strength E but mobility U = v/E depends on charge density and shape, which native PAGE does not normalize, so no voltage change makes native migration track mass.  
**Distractor citation 2:** Choice C (adjacent technique): Line 7982: 'Isoelectric Focusing (IEF): Separates proteins based on their isoelectric points (pI) by establishing a pH gradient' — separates by pI, not molecular mass.  
**Distractor citation 3:** Choice D (partial truth): Lines 7499-7505: 'A multimeric protein whose subunits are held together by disulfide bonds can be resolved into component subunits by adding a reducing agent. If the subunits are held together only by noncovalent interactions, they will separate under denaturing conditions (SDS)' — a reducing agent cleaves disulfides but, absent SDS, does not normalize charge density, so mass cannot be read from standards.

### Q11 — Isoelectric Focusing (hard, Skill 2)
**Stem:** A researcher runs isoelectric focusing on a single purified protein whose isoelectric point (pI) has been measured as 5.2. The gel strip carries a stable linear pH gradient running from pH 3.0 at the left end (positioned at the anode, +) to pH 10.0 at the right end (positioned at the cathode, −). The protein sample is loaded into a well at the position where the local gel pH is 8.0, and a voltage is then applied until the bands stop moving. When the run is stopped, at what final position does the protein band form, and why did it move in that direction from the loading well?  
**Answer:** A · **Foundation:** 5C · **Confidence:** 5  
**Answer citation:** Lines 7811-7814: "Proteins initially in regions with a pH below their isoelectric point are positively charged and migrate toward the cathode... The migration will lead to a region where the pH matches the pI. There, the protein will have a net charge of zero and stop." And lines 5560-5563: "As pH increases, the most acidic groups will begin to deprotonate, and the net charge will become less positive... the overall charge of the protein will be negative. At some pH, then, the net charge will be 0. This pH is called the isoelectric point (pI)."  
**Distractor citation 1:** Choice B (reversed direction): Lines 7811-7812 establish charge depends on pH relative to pI — "Proteins initially in regions with a pH below their isoelectric point are positively charged and migrate toward the cathode" — so above the pI the sign and direction reverse; a pH of 8.0 (above pI 5.2) yields net negative, contradicting the net-positive/cathode claim.  
**Distractor citation 2:** Choice C (field cannot move it): Lines 7813-7815 state the protein moves and only stops where "the pH matches the pI. There, the protein will have a net charge of zero and stop... molecules will always move to their pI region," so a protein at pH 8.0 (not its pI) is charged and does migrate.  
**Distractor citation 3:** Choice D (runs to the extreme low-pH end): Lines 7813-7814 specify it stops at its pI, not at an electrode — "amphoteric molecules are located in narrow bands where the pI coincides with the pH," refuting migration all the way to pH 3.0.

### Q15 — Chromatography (medium, Skill 1)

• Protein X: pI = 6.5
• Protein Y: pI = 8.0
• Protein Z: pI = 9.5

The proteins differ only in surface charge; assume no other interactions with the resin. Which protein requires the highest salt concentration to elute (i.e., has the longest retention on the column)?  
**Answer:** D · **Foundation:** 5C · **Confidence:** 5  
**Answer citation:** Lines 7010-7013: "Upon sample injection, solute molecules will exchange with the buffer ions as each competes for the binding sites on the resin. Line 7003: "The most positively charged protein will be eluted last, at the highest salt concentration."  
**Distractor citation 1:** Choice A (Protein W, net negative, does not bind): Lines 7000-7001: "In contrast, the negatively charged proteins will pass through the column." Lines 6993-6995: "positively charged proteins will bind to a resin containing negatively charged groups, like the carboxymethyl group, CM (-OCH2COO-)" — establishes that only net-positive proteins bind the CM cation exchanger, so W's low pI (net negative at pH 6.0) means no retention.  
**Distractor citation 2:** Choice B (Protein X, weakest positive charge elutes first): Line 7012: "The most weakly charged compounds will elute first" — the protein whose pI is nearest the buffer pH carries the weakest charge and therefore has the shortest, not longest, retention.  
**Distractor citation 3:** Choice C (Protein Y, intermediate charge): Lines 7012-7013: "followed by those with successively stronger charges" — establishes a graded order in which an intermediate net charge elutes after weaker ones but before the strongest, so Y is not last.
