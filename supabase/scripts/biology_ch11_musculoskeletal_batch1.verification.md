## Q1 — Muscle Types & Characteristics · **easy** (topic easy / structure easy) · Skill 1 · 75s · confidence 5
**Stem:** A histology student examines a stained skeletal muscle fiber under the light microscope and notes that it contains many nuclei pressed against the inner edge of the cell membrane. Which feature of this fiber most directly accounts for this nuclear arrangement?

- **A.** Fusion of many myoblasts, whose nuclei remain at the periphery ✅
- **B.** A single, centrally located nucleus from one myocyte
- **C.** Failure of precursor myoblasts to fuse, leaving one nucleus
- **D.** Myoblast nuclei migrating to the fiber's central core

**Correct (A) citation:** openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt lines 18210-18212: "As a myotube is formed from many different myoblast cells, it contains many nuclei, but has a continuous cytoplasm. This is why skeletal muscle cells are multinucleate, as the nucleus of each contributing myoblast remains intact in the mature skeletal muscle cell." and line 7135: "Skeletal muscle cells have prominent striation and nuclei on their periphery."

**Distractors:**
- **B** — _adjacent_fact_: A single centrally located nucleus is the defining feature of a cardiomyocyte, not a skeletal muscle fiber, which has many nuclei displaced to the periphery; this choice swaps the correct muscle type.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt lines 7113-7114: "Unlike skeletal muscle fibers, cardiomyocytes are single cells typically with a single centrally located nucleus."
- **C** — _reversed_relationship_: Retaining one nucleus because precursor cells fail to fuse describes cardiac and smooth muscle; skeletal muscle is multinucleated precisely because its myoblasts DO fuse, so this reverses the causal relationship.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt line 18221: "cells are not multinucleate because the myoblasts that form their cells do not fuse."
- **D** — _process_step_confusion_: After fusion the conserved myoblast nuclei stay at the periphery of the fiber; they do not migrate to a central core, so this misstates the final step and contradicts the observed peripheral arrangement.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt line 7135: "Skeletal muscle cells have prominent striation and nuclei on their periphery." and lines 18211-18212: "the nucleus of each contributing myoblast remains intact in the mature skeletal muscle cell."

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Organ Systems.' The answer is A because a skeletal muscle fiber is multinucleated with peripheral nuclei as a direct result of the fusion of many myoblasts whose individual nuclei are conserved at the cell's edge. During development, many smaller myoblast cells fuse to form a mature muscle fiber, and the nucleus of each contributing myoblast remains intact in the mature cell, imparting the multinucleate characteristic (lines 5045-5046, 18210-18212). Under the light microscope these fibers appear striated with many nuclei squeezed along the membranes, that is, on their periphery (lines 7107, 7135). The continuous shared cytoplasm of the fused syncytium leaves the conserved nuclei displaced toward the sarcolemma rather than gathered centrally. (Choice A) is correct: myoblast fusion conserves each precursor's nucleus, and these many nuclei sit at the periphery of the long fiber, which is exactly the arrangement observed. (Choice B) describes a cardiomyocyte, which is a single cell typically with a single centrally located nucleus (lines 7113-7114), not the many peripheral nuclei of a skeletal fiber. (Choice C) describes smooth and cardiac muscle, whose cells are not multinucleate because the myoblasts that form them do not fuse (line 18221); a skeletal fiber instead results from extensive fusion. (Choice D) reverses the actual outcome of fusion: the conserved myoblast nuclei remain at the periphery of the fiber, not migrating to a central core (lines 7135, 18211-18212). This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall that skeletal muscle fibers are multinucleated with peripheral nuclei as a direct consequence of myoblast fusion.

**Stem:** A researcher isolates a single cardiac muscle cell and observes that it contains only one centrally located nucleus, unlike a neighboring skeletal muscle fiber that contains many nuclei. Which developmental event most directly accounts for the single nucleus of the cardiac muscle cell?

- **A.** Its nuclei migrate inward and merge into one
- **B.** Its myoblasts develop without fusing together ✅
- **C.** Its myoblasts fuse to form a syncytium
- **D.** It loses extra nuclei after maturation

**Correct (B) citation:** /Users/mikkonieveras/praxis-prep/reference-textbooks/biology/openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 18212, 18221: "However, cardiac and smooth muscle ... cells are not multinucleate because the myoblasts that form their cells do not fuse."

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _misconception_: The textbook never describes nuclei migrating inward and merging; cardiac cells are uninucleate because their myoblasts do not fuse, not because multiple nuclei combine into one.  
  ↳ /Users/mikkonieveras/praxis-prep/reference-textbooks/biology/openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, line 18221: "cells are not multinucleate because the myoblasts that form their cells do not fuse."
- **C** — _reversed_relationship_: Myoblast fusion into a syncytium is the mechanism that makes skeletal muscle multinucleate, the opposite of cardiac muscle; it would produce many nuclei, not a single one.  
  ↳ /Users/mikkonieveras/praxis-prep/reference-textbooks/biology/openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 18210-18211: "As a myotube is formed from many different myoblast cells, it contains many nuclei ... This is why skeletal muscle cells are multinucleate"
- **D** — _process_step_confusion_: Cardiac cells are not initially multinucleate and do not lose nuclei after maturation; they remain uninucleate from the start because no myoblast fusion occurs.  
  ↳ /Users/mikkonieveras/praxis-prep/reference-textbooks/biology/openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 18017-18018: "cardiac muscle fibers ... usually contain only one nucleus, which is located in the central region of the cell."

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Organ Systems.' The answer is B because cardiac muscle cells retain a single nucleus since the myoblasts that form them do not fuse, unlike the fusing myoblasts that make skeletal muscle multinucleate. During development, a myoblast is a muscle-forming stem cell, and in skeletal muscle many myoblasts fuse into a syncytium (myotube) in which the nucleus of each contributing myoblast remains intact, producing a multinucleate fiber. By contrast, the textbook states that cardiac and smooth muscle cells are not multinucleate because the myoblasts that form their cells do not fuse, so each mature cardiac cell keeps the single nucleus of one myoblast. The single nucleus of cardiac muscle is therefore the default outcome of myoblasts that never combine, not the result of any merging or loss of nuclei. (Choice A) Nuclei do not migrate inward and merge into one; cardiac muscle simply never accumulates multiple nuclei because its myoblasts do not fuse, and nuclear merging is not a described mechanism. (Choice B) This is correct: cardiac (and smooth) muscle cells are uninucleate precisely because their myoblasts do not fuse, leaving each cell with the single nucleus of its founding myoblast. (Choice C) Fusion of myoblasts into a syncytium is exactly what produces the multinucleate skeletal muscle fiber, the opposite of what happens in cardiac muscle, so it would yield many nuclei rather than one. (Choice D) Cardiac cells do not start multinucleate and then shed nuclei after maturation; they begin and remain uninucleate because no fusion occurs in the first place. This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall that cardiac and smooth muscle remain uninucleate because their myoblasts do not fuse, whereas skeletal muscle is multinucleate due to myoblast fusion.

## Q3 — Muscle Types & Characteristics · **easy** (topic easy / structure easy) · Skill 1 · 75s · confidence 5
**Stem:** A histologist examines a tissue sample from the wall of the small intestine and notes that the spindle-shaped cells contain a single nucleus and show a uniform, nonstriated appearance under the light microscope. Which feature of these cells most directly accounts for the absence of visible striations?

- **A.** The cells contain no actin or myosin filaments
- **B.** The cells lack troponin to bind \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\)
- **C.** The actin and myosin are not arranged in sarcomeres ✅
- **D.** The dense bodies have replaced all thick filaments

**Correct (C) citation:** openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt lines 17090-17091: "Because the actin and myosin are not arranged in such regular fashion in smooth muscle, the cytoplasm of a smooth muscle fiber (which has only a single nucleus) has a uniform, nonstriated appearance (resulting in the name smooth muscle)." Also lines 18105-18106: "Although they do not have striations and sarcomeres, smooth muscle fibers do have actin and myosin contractile proteins, and thick and thin filaments."

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _misconception_: Smooth muscle fibers do contain actin and myosin contractile proteins and thick and thin filaments; the absence of these filaments is not the reason striations are absent.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt line 18106: "Although they do not have striations and sarcomeres, smooth muscle fibers do have actin and myosin contractile proteins, and thick and thin filaments."
- **B** — _adjacent_fact_: Smooth muscle does lack troponin, but this explains why contraction is regulated by calmodulin instead of the troponin-tropomyosin complex; it does not determine whether striations are visible.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt lines 18111-18112: "Because smooth muscle cells do not contain troponin, cross-bridge formation is not regulated by the troponin-tropomyosin complex but instead by the regulatory protein calmodulin."
- **D** — _partial_truth_: Dense bodies are analogous to the Z-discs of striated muscle and anchor thin filaments, but they do not replace thick filaments, which are still present in smooth muscle, so this does not explain the lack of striations.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt lines 18106-18107: "smooth muscle fibers do have actin and myosin contractile proteins, and thick and thin filaments. These thin filaments are anchored by dense bodies. A dense body is analogous to the Z-discs of skeletal and cardiac muscle fibers."

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Organ Systems.' The answer is C because smooth muscle appears nonstriated since its actin and myosin contractile proteins are not arranged in the regular, banded sarcomere pattern seen in skeletal and cardiac muscle. Striations are the visible stripes produced when actin and myosin are organized in a highly regular, repeating register that a light microscope can resolve, as occurs in the sarcomeres of skeletal and cardiac fibers. Because the actin and myosin are not arranged in such a regular fashion in smooth muscle, the cytoplasm of a smooth muscle fiber has a uniform, nonstriated appearance, which gives smooth muscle its name. Smooth muscle fibers do still contain actin and myosin and thick and thin filaments, but these lack the sarcomere register required to produce stripes. (Choice A) Smooth muscle fibers do have actin and myosin contractile proteins and thick and thin filaments; their presence is not what is missing, so the absence of striations cannot be due to a lack of these filaments. (Choice B) Smooth muscle does lack troponin, but this absence explains why cross-bridge formation is regulated by calmodulin rather than the troponin-tropomyosin complex, not why the fiber appears nonstriated. (Choice C) Is correct: the lack of a regular sarcomere arrangement of actin and myosin is precisely why no banded striations are visible. (Choice D) Dense bodies anchor thin filaments and are analogous to the Z-discs of striated muscle, but they do not replace thick filaments, which are still present in smooth muscle. This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall that striations arise from the regular sarcomere arrangement of actin and myosin and that smooth muscle lacks this organization.

## Q4 — Muscle Types & Characteristics · **easy** (topic easy / structure easy) · Skill 1 · 75s · confidence 5
**Stem:** A researcher isolates a single skeletal muscle fiber from the quadriceps and bathes it in a physiological solution. To make this fiber contract, the stimulus must most directly come from which source?

- **A.** A circulating hormone reaching the fiber
- **B.** The fiber's own intrinsic rhythmic activity
- **C.** Mechanical stretch of the fiber membrane
- **D.** A somatic motor neuron firing at the neuromuscular junction ✅

**Correct (D) citation:** openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt lines 17215-17218: "the site where a motor neuron's terminal meets the muscle fiber-called the neuromuscular junction (NMJ). ... Every skeletal muscle fiber in every skeletal muscle is innervated by a motor neuron at the NMJ. Excitation signals from the neuron are the only way to functionally activate the fiber to contract." Also lines 17255-17256: "In skeletal muscle, this sequence begins with signals from the somatic motor division of the nervous system. In other words, the 'excitation' step in skeletal muscles is always triggered by signaling from the nervous system."

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _adjacent_fact_: Hormonal stimulation activates cardiac and smooth muscle, not skeletal muscle; skeletal muscle has no hormonal activation pathway and depends only on neural excitation.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt line 17055: "both cardiac muscle and smooth muscle can respond to other stimuli, such as hormones and local stimuli." Contrast line 17054: "skeletal muscle completely depends on signaling from the nervous system to work properly."
- **B** — _misconception_: Self-generated intrinsic rhythm is a property of cardiomyocytes, not skeletal muscle fibers; skeletal fibers cannot contract without external neural excitation.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt lines 7114-7115: "A principal characteristic of cardiomyocytes is that they contract on their own intrinsic rhythms without any external stimulation."
- **C** — _adjacent_fact_: Mechanical stretch triggers smooth muscle (the stress-relaxation response), but it does not functionally activate a skeletal muscle fiber, which still requires somatic motor neuron excitation.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt line 18159: "In certain locations, such as the walls of visceral organs, stretching the muscle can trigger its contraction (the stress-relaxation response)." This applies to smooth muscle, contrasted with line 17153: "the only way to functionally contract a skeletal muscle is through signaling from the nervous system."

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Organ Systems.' The answer is D because excitation signals delivered by a somatic motor neuron at the neuromuscular junction (NMJ) are the only way to functionally activate a skeletal muscle fiber to contract. Every skeletal muscle fiber is innervated by a motor neuron at the NMJ, and the textbook states that "Excitation signals from the neuron are the only way to functionally activate the fiber to contract" (lines 17217-17218). Unlike cardiac and smooth muscle, skeletal muscle "completely depends on signaling from the nervous system to work properly," so the "excitation" step in skeletal muscle is always triggered by the somatic motor division of the nervous system (lines 17054, 17255-17256). The voluntary movements skeletal muscle produces therefore require neural drive routed through the NMJ. (Choice A) A circulating hormone can trigger cardiac and smooth muscle, which "can respond to other stimuli, such as hormones and local stimuli," but skeletal muscle has no such hormonal activation pathway. (Choice B) Intrinsic rhythmic activity describes cardiomyocytes, which "contract on their own intrinsic rhythms without any external stimulation"-a property skeletal muscle fibers lack. (Choice C) Mechanical stretch can trigger smooth muscle (the stress-relaxation response), but stretch does not functionally activate a skeletal muscle fiber, which still requires neural excitation. (Choice D) A somatic motor neuron firing at the NMJ is correct because this is the sole functional route to skeletal muscle contraction; without this neural excitation the fiber cannot contract regardless of other conditions in the bath. This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall that skeletal muscle is voluntary and depends exclusively on somatic motor neuron excitation at the NMJ for activation.

## Q5 — Muscle Types & Characteristics · **easy** (topic easy / structure easy) · Skill 1 · 75s · confidence 5
**Stem:** A histologist examines a stained tissue sample and observes short, branched fibers, each with a single central nucleus, joined to neighboring fibers at their ends by distinct transverse bands. The transverse band joining the fibers end-to-end most likely identifies the tissue as which muscle type?

- **A.** Cardiac muscle ✅
- **B.** Skeletal muscle
- **C.** Single-unit smooth muscle
- **D.** Multiunit smooth muscle

**Correct (A) citation:** openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt lines 18019-18021: "Cardiac muscle fibers cells also are extensively branched and are connected to one another at their ends by intercalated discs. An intercalated disc allows the cardiac muscle cells to contract in a wave-like pattern so that the heart can work as a pump." and lines 18035-18036: "Intercalated discs are part of the sarcolemma and contain two structures important in cardiac muscle contraction: gap junctions and desmosomes."

**Distractors:**
- **B** — _misconception_: Skeletal muscle fibers are long, unbranched, and multinucleated, so they are not joined to one another by intercalated discs; the described short, branched, single-nucleus fibers with end-to-end junctions cannot be skeletal.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt line 18408: "Skeletal muscle fibers are long, multinucleated cells."
- **C** — _adjacent_fact_: Single-unit smooth muscle fibers are joined by gap junctions so the tissue contracts as one unit, but those gap junctions are not packaged into intercalated discs, and the cells are spindle-shaped with a single nucleus rather than branched.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt line 18177: "Single-unit muscle has its muscle fibers joined by gap junctions so that the muscle contracts as a single unit." and line 18103: "Smooth muscle fibers are spindle-shaped (wide in the middle and tapered at both ends, somewhat like a football) and have a single nucleus"

  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt line 18188: "Multiunit smooth muscle cells rarely possess gap junctions, and thus are not electrically coupled."

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Organ Systems.' The answer is A because the transverse bands joining fibers end-to-end are intercalated discs, structures found only in cardiac muscle. Cardiac muscle fibers are short, branched, usually contain a single central nucleus, and are connected to one another at their ends by intercalated discs, which contain gap junctions and desmosomes that let the cells contract in a coordinated wave-like pattern so the heart works as a pump (Anatomy & Physiology 2e, lines 18019-18021, 18035-18036). No other muscle type uses intercalated discs to join its cells end-to-end. (Choice A) Cardiac muscle is correct: the combination of short, branched, single-nucleus fibers joined end-to-end by intercalated discs is diagnostic of cardiac tissue, which is found only in the heart. (Choice B) Skeletal muscle is wrong because its fibers are long, unbranched, and multinucleated, and they are not joined to each other by intercalated discs. (Choice C) Single-unit smooth muscle is wrong because, although its fibers are joined by gap junctions so the muscle contracts as a single unit, those junctions are not packaged into intercalated discs and its cells are spindle-shaped, non-striated, and single-nucleated rather than branched. (Choice D) Multiunit smooth muscle is wrong because its cells rarely possess gap junctions, are not electrically coupled, and likewise lack intercalated discs. This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall that intercalated discs are the end-to-end junctions unique to cardiac muscle.

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _misconception_: Cardiac muscle is involuntary and cannot be consciously controlled; voluntary control is a property of skeletal muscle, not the heart, so it cannot explain a denervated heart's continued beating.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, line 18059: "Although cardiac muscle cannot be consciously controlled, the pacemaker cells respond to signals from the autonomic nervous system (ANS)"; glossary line 18305: "Cardiac muscle is under involuntary control."
- **C** — _adjacent_fact_: Complete dependence on nervous signaling describes skeletal muscle, not cardiac muscle; if cardiac muscle depended entirely on nerves it could not keep beating after denervation, which is the opposite of the observed result.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, line 17054: "skeletal muscle completely depends on signaling from the nervous system to work properly."
- **D** — _reversed_relationship_: The autonomic nervous system only modulates heart rate (speeds it up or slows it down); it does not initiate each beat. The pacemaker cells initiate beats intrinsically, so a severed-nerve heart still contracts.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 18059-18060: "the pacemaker cells respond to signals from the autonomic nervous system (ANS) to speed up or slow down the heart rate."

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Organ Systems.' The answer is B because specialized cardiac pacemaker cells are self-excitable and fire action potentials on their own at set intervals, so the heart keeps beating even with every nerve cut. The textbook states that the wave of contraction begins with pacemaker cells, a group that is "self-excitable and able to depolarize to threshold and fire action potentials on their own, a feature called autorhythmicity; they do this at set intervals which determine heart rate" (A&P 2e, lines 18063-18065). (Choice A) Conscious voluntary control is wrong because cardiac muscle is involuntary—it "cannot be consciously controlled" (line 18059)—and voluntary control is a feature of skeletal muscle, not the heart. (Choice B) Autorhythmicity is correct: it is precisely the named property that lets pacemaker cells generate beats intrinsically, which is why a denervated heart still contracts. (Choice C) Complete dependence on nervous signaling is wrong because that describes skeletal muscle, which "completely depends on signaling from the nervous system to work properly" (line 17054); cardiac muscle does not, which is the whole reason it keeps beating after denervation. (Choice D) Initiation by the autonomic nervous system is a reversed relationship: the ANS only modulates the rate, sending "signals... to speed up or slow down the heart rate" (lines 18059-18060), but it does not initiate each beat—the pacemaker cells do that themselves. This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall that cardiac muscle is involuntary yet contracts on an intrinsic rhythm generated by self-excitable pacemaker cells (autorhythmicity), independent of nervous input.

## Q7 — Muscle Types & Characteristics · **medium** (topic medium / structure medium) · Skill 2 · 105s · confidence 5
**Stem:** A researcher treats a sheet of cultured cardiac muscle cells with a drug that selectively blocks the gap junctions within their intercalated discs, while leaving the desmosomes intact. When one cell is then stimulated to threshold, which consequence is most likely to follow?

- **A.** Adjacent cells pull apart during contraction
- **B.** Action potentials no longer reach the cell interior
- **C.** Depolarizing current no longer spreads to neighboring cells ✅
- **D.** Calcium is no longer released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum

**Correct (C) citation:** openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt lines 18036-18040: "A gap junction forms channels between adjacent cardiac muscle fibers that allow the depolarizing current produced by cations to flow from one cardiac muscle cell to the next. This joining is called electric coupling, and in cardiac muscle it allows the quick transmission of action potentials and the coordinated contraction of the entire heart. This network of electrically connected cardiac muscle cells creates a functional unit of contraction called a syncytium."

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _misconception_: Preventing cells from pulling apart is the mechanical role of desmosomes, not gap junctions; the drug leaves desmosomes intact, so the cells stay anchored. This swaps the electrical function of gap junctions for the structural function of the other intercalated disc component.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt lines 18040-18042: "A desmosome is a cell structure that anchors the ends of cardiac muscle fibers together so the cells do not pull apart during the stress of individual fibers contracting"
- **B** — _adjacent_fact_: Carrying the action potential into the interior of a single cell is the role of T-tubules, an intracellular structure independent of the intercellular gap junctions, so internal depolarization of the stimulated cell is unaffected.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt lines 17308-17309: "The T-tubules carry the action potential into the interior of the cell, which triggers the opening of calcium channels in the membrane of the adjacent SR"
- **D** — _process_step_confusion_: Release of calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum is a downstream intracellular step triggered within the stimulated cell itself, not a step that requires gap junctions, so the directly stimulated cell still releases its calcium.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt lines 17308-17310: "triggers the opening of calcium channels in the membrane of the adjacent SR, causing Ca++ to diffuse out of the SR and into the sarcoplasm. It is the arrival of Ca++ in the sarcoplasm that initiates contraction"

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Organ Systems.' The answer is C because gap junctions in the intercalated discs are the channels that let depolarizing current pass directly from one cardiac muscle cell to the next, so blocking them isolates the stimulated cell electrically. The textbook states that "A gap junction forms channels between adjacent cardiac muscle fibers that allow the depolarizing current produced by cations to flow from one cardiac muscle cell to the next," and that this "electric coupling... allows the quick transmission of action potentials and the coordinated contraction of the entire heart," producing "a functional unit of contraction called a syncytium." Remove the gap junctions and the cations carrying the depolarizing current can no longer flow cell-to-cell, so the wave of synchronized contraction cannot spread even though the desmosomes still hold the cells together. (Choice A) Anchoring the cells so they "do not pull apart during the stress of individual fibers contracting" is the job of the desmosomes, which the drug leaves intact, so mechanical separation is not the expected result; this confuses the electrical role of gap junctions with the mechanical role of the other intercalated disc structure. (Choice B) Carrying the action potential "into the interior of the cell" is the function of the T-tubules within a single fiber, an event independent of the intercellular gap junctions, so a stimulated cell still depolarizes internally. (Choice C) is correct, and note that with the syncytium electrically uncoupled, only the one stimulated cell would contract rather than the tissue acting as a coordinated unit. (Choice D) Release of \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) from the sarcoplasmic reticulum is a downstream intracellular step of excitation-contraction coupling triggered within the stimulated cell itself, not something that depends on gap junctions, so the directly stimulated cell still releases its \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) and contracts. This is a Scientific Reasoning and Problem Solving question because you must apply the role of intercalated disc gap junctions in electrically coupling cardiomyocytes to predict the outcome of selectively blocking them in an experimental preparation.
