**Stem:** An action potential travels down the axon of a somatic motor neuron and reaches the axon terminal at the neuromuscular junction of a skeletal muscle fiber. Which chemical messenger is most directly released into the synaptic cleft to bind receptors on the motor end-plate?

- **B.** Acetylcholinesterase
- **C.** Calcium ions
- **D.** Norepinephrine

**Correct (A) citation:** /Users/mikkonieveras/praxis-prep/reference-textbooks/biology/openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt lines 17276-17278: "At the NMJ, the axon terminal releases a chemical messenger, or neurotransmitter, called acetylcholine (ACh). - **B** — _adjacent_fact_: Acetylcholinesterase is the enzyme that DEGRADES acetylcholine in the synaptic cleft to terminate the signal, not the messenger released by the axon terminal to bind the motor end-plate.  
  ↳ /Users/mikkonieveras/praxis-prep/reference-textbooks/biology/openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt lines 17289-17291: "the ACh in the synaptic cleft is degraded by the enzyme acetylcholinesterase (AChE) so that the ACh cannot rebind to a receptor and reopen its channel, which would cause unwanted extended muscle excitation and contraction."

  ↳ /Users/mikkonieveras/praxis-prep/reference-textbooks/biology/openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt lines 22225-22228: "When an action potential reaches the axon terminals, voltage-gated Ca2+ channels in the membrane of the synaptic end bulb open. The concentration of Ca2+ increases inside the end bulb ... - **D** — _misconception_: Norepinephrine is an adrenergic biogenic-amine transmitter found at autonomic/adrenergic sites and released as a hormone, not the transmitter at the somatic skeletal neuromuscular junction, where acetylcholine is used.  
  ↳ /Users/mikkonieveras/praxis-prep/reference-textbooks/biology/openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt lines 22283-22290: "Other biogenic amines are made from tyrosine, and include dopamine, norepinephrine, and epinephrine ... **Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Organ Systems.' The answer is A because the axon terminal of a somatic motor neuron releases acetylcholine into the synaptic cleft, where it binds receptors on the motor end-plate to begin excitation. The textbook states, "At the NMJ, the axon terminal releases a chemical messenger, or neurotransmitter, called acetylcholine (ACh). The ACh molecules diffuse across a minute space called the synaptic cleft and bind to ACh receptors located within the motor end-plate of the sarcolemma" (lines 17276-17278). The source also lists, for the NMJ, that "the neurotransmitter is acetylcholine" (line 22219). Binding opens a receptor channel that lets positively charged ions enter and depolarize the fiber, initiating excitation-contraction coupling. (Choice A) Acetylcholine is correct: it is the neurotransmitter packaged in vesicles in the presynaptic terminal and exocytosed into the cleft to act on the end-plate. (Choice B) Acetylcholinesterase is the enzyme that degrades ACh in the cleft so it "cannot rebind to a receptor and reopen its channel" (lines 17289-17291); it terminates the signal rather than being the released messenger. (Choice C) Calcium ions are the intracellular trigger inside the terminal — when the action potential arrives, "voltage-gated Ca2+ channels in the membrane of the synaptic end bulb open" and \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) "facilitates the merging of the vesicle with the presynaptic membrane" (lines 22225-22228), so \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) causes ACh release but is not itself the messenger crossing the cleft to the end-plate. This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall that the somatic motor neuron releases acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction to depolarize the motor end-plate.

## Q2 — Excitation-Contraction Coupling · **easy** (topic easy/struct easy) · Skill 1 · 75s · conf 5
**Stem:** A motor neuron releases acetylcholine into the synaptic cleft, and the \(\text{ACh}\) binds receptors in the motor end-plate of a skeletal muscle fiber. What happens at the end-plate most directly as a result of this binding?

- **A.** \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) is released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum into the cytosol
- **B.** \(\text{Na}^+\) enters the fiber and depolarizes the sarcolemma ✅
- **C.** \(\text{K}^+\) exits the fiber and repolarizes the sarcolemma
- **D.** Acetylcholinesterase degrades \(\text{ACh}\) in the synaptic cleft

**Correct (B) citation:** openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 17279-17285: "Once ACh binds, a channel in the ACh receptor opens and positively charged ions can pass through into the muscle fiber, causing it to depolarize, meaning that the membrane potential of the muscle fiber becomes less negative (closer to zero.) As the membrane depolarizes, another set of ion channels called voltage-gated sodium channels are triggered to open. Sodium ions enter the muscle fiber, and an action potential rapidly spreads (or "fires") along the entire membrane to initiate excitation-contraction coupling." Also lines 22300-22302: "When acetylcholine binds to the nicotinic receptor, the postsynaptic cell is depolarized. This is because the receptor is a cation channel and positively charged Na+ will rush into the cell."

- **A** — _process_step_confusion_: SR calcium release is a real EC-coupling step but a later one: it is triggered by the action potential propagating along the sarcolemma, not directly by ACh binding at the end-plate, so it is the wrong step in the sequence.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 17293-17294: "Propagation of an action potential along the sarcolemma is the excitation portion of excitation-contraction coupling. Recall that this excitation actually triggers the release of calcium ions (Ca++) from its storage in the cell's SR."
- **C** — _reversed_relationship_: This reverses both ion direction and outcome: repolarization re-establishes the negative potential and occurs immediately after depolarization, the opposite of the inward-cation depolarizing event produced by ACh binding.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 17288-17289: "Immediately following depolarization of the membrane, it repolarizes, re-establishing the negative membrane potential."
- **D** — _adjacent_fact_: Acetylcholinesterase is a real participant at the NMJ, but it terminates signaling by degrading ACh so it cannot rebind; it does not produce the depolarization that ACh binding causes.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 17289-17291: "the ACh in the synaptic cleft is degraded by the enzyme acetylcholinesterase (AChE) so that the ACh cannot rebind to a receptor and reopen its channel, which would cause unwanted extended muscle excitation and contraction."

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Organ Systems.' The answer is B because \(\text{ACh}\) binding opens a cation channel in the end-plate receptor, letting positively charged \(\text{Na}^+\) flow into the fiber and depolarize the sarcolemma. The textbook states that "Once ACh binds, a channel in the ACh receptor opens and positively charged ions can pass through into the muscle fiber, causing it to depolarize, meaning that the membrane potential of the muscle fiber becomes less negative." Because this receptor is a cation channel, "positively charged Na+ will rush into the cell," driving the membrane toward threshold so that voltage-gated sodium channels then open and an action potential fires. This local end-plate depolarization is the initiating electrical event of excitation-contraction coupling. (Choice A) \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum is a real EC-coupling step, but it occurs later: the action potential propagating along the sarcolemma "triggers the release of calcium ions (Ca++) from its storage in the cell's SR," so SR calcium release is downstream of end-plate depolarization, not the direct result of \(\text{ACh}\) binding. (Choice B) is correct: \(\text{ACh}\) opens a cation channel and inward \(\text{Na}^+\) depolarizes the end-plate, the first step toward the muscle action potential. (Choice C) reverses both the ion movement and the outcome; \(\text{K}^+\) efflux producing repolarization is the opposite event, occurring "immediately following depolarization of the membrane," when "it repolarizes, re-establishing the negative membrane potential." (Choice D) acetylcholinesterase does act at this synapse, but it degrades \(\text{ACh}\) "so that the ACh cannot rebind to a receptor," terminating signaling rather than producing the depolarization. This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall that \(\text{ACh}\) binding opens a cation channel allowing \(\text{Na}^+\) influx that depolarizes the sarcolemma.

## Q3 — Excitation-Contraction Coupling · **easy** (topic easy/struct easy) · Skill 1 · 75s · conf 5
**Stem:** A researcher uses a detergent to selectively detach the T-tubules from the rest of the sarcolemma in an isolated skeletal muscle fiber, leaving the surface membrane and sarcoplasmic reticulum otherwise intact. When the surface sarcolemma is then stimulated to fire an action potential, the deep myofibrils in the fiber's interior most likely fail to contract because the action potential is no longer carried to the interior to trigger which event?

- **B.** Reuptake of \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) into the sarcoplasmic reticulum

**Correct (C) citation:** openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 17308–17310: "The T-tubules carry the action potential into the interior of the cell, which triggers the opening of calcium channels in the membrane of the adjacent SR, causing Ca++ to diffuse out of the SR and into the sarcoplasm. It is the arrival of Ca++ in the sarcoplasm that initiates contraction of the muscle fiber by its contractile units, or sarcomeres."

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _process_step_confusion_: Acetylcholine release happens upstream at the neuromuscular junction, before the action potential travels along the sarcolemma and T-tubules; it is a motor-neuron event, not the interior event the T-tubule action potential triggers.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 17278–17285: "synaptic cleft and bind to ACh receptors located within the motor end-plate of the sarcolemma on the other side of ... membrane to initiate excitation-contraction coupling." and lines 17293–17294: "Propagation of an action potential along the sarcolemma is the excitation portion of excitation-contraction coupling. Recall that this excitation actually triggers the release of calcium ions (Ca++) from its storage in the cell's SR."
- **B** — _reversed_relationship_: Reuptake of Ca2+ into the SR is a relaxation event that occurs when motor neuron signaling ends—the opposite of the Ca2+ release that the incoming action potential triggers.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 17343–17345: "Muscle contraction usually stops when signaling from the motor neuron ends, which repolarizes the sarcolemma and T-tubules, and closes the voltage-gated calcium channels in the SR. Ca++ ions are then pumped back into the SR."
- **D** — _partial_truth_: Ca2+ binding to troponin is a real and necessary step, but it is downstream: it can only occur after Ca2+ has already been released from the SR into the sarcoplasm, so it is not the event the T-tubule action potential directly triggers.

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Organ Systems.' The answer is C because the T-tubules carry the action potential into the cell's interior, where it triggers the opening of calcium channels in the membrane of the adjacent sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR). The textbook states, "The T-tubules carry the action potential into the interior of the cell, which triggers the opening of calcium channels in the membrane of the adjacent SR, causing Ca++ to diffuse out of the SR and into the sarcoplasm" (lines 17308–17310). T-tubules are "periodic invaginations in the sarcolemma" that exist precisely so "the action potential [can] reach the membrane of the SR," ensuring "the membrane can get close to the SR in the sarcoplasm" deep inside a fiber up to 100 μm wide (lines 17294–17297). If the T-tubules are detached, the depolarization cannot propagate to the SR in the interior, so the SR \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) channels never open and the deep myofibrils receive no \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) signal. (Choice A) Acetylcholine release occurs upstream at the neuromuscular junction from the motor neuron's axon terminal, before any action potential travels along the sarcolemma, so it is not the event the T-tubules deliver the impulse to trigger (lines 17275–17285). (Choice B) Reuptake of \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) into the SR is part of relaxation—"Ca++ ions are then pumped back into the SR"—which happens when signaling stops, the reverse of the release event a T-tubule action potential triggers (lines 17343–17345). (Choice C) is correct because the arrival of the action potential at the SR via the T-tubules is the direct trigger for SR \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) channel opening and \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) efflux into the sarcoplasm. (Choice D) \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) binding to troponin is a downstream consequence that can only occur after \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) has already been released from the SR into the sarcoplasm, so it is not the event the T-tubule directly triggers (lines 17332–17334). This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall that T-tubules conduct the action potential into the fiber interior to trigger SR calcium-channel opening.

## Q5 — Cross-Bridge Cycle & Sliding Filament · **easy** (topic easy/struct easy) · Skill 1 · 70s · conf 5
**Stem:** During sustained skeletal muscle contraction, a single myosin head repeatedly attaches to actin, executes a power stroke, detaches, and re-cocks. Which molecule does the myosin head directly hydrolyze to power each round of this cross-bridge cycle?

- **A.** \(\text{ATP}\) ✅
- **B.** Creatine phosphate
- **C.** \(\text{ADP}\)
- **D.** \(\text{GTP}\)

**Correct (A) citation:** openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 17439-17441: "ATP binding causes the myosin head to detach from the actin (Figure 10.11d). After this occurs, ATP is converted to ADP and Pi by the intrinsic ATPase activity of myosin. The energy released during ATP hydrolysis changes the angle of the myosin head into a cocked position"; and lines 17409-17411: "Each cycle requires energy, and the action of the myosin heads in the sarcomeres repetitively pulling on the thin filaments also requires energy, which is provided by ATP."

**Distractors:**
- **B** — _partial_truth_: Creatine phosphate is an energy reserve that regenerates ATP by donating its phosphate to ADP; it is never directly hydrolyzed by the myosin head. It is correct only as an upstream ATP-replenishing source, not the molecule split during cross-bridge cycling.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 17466-17469: "This acts as an energy reserve that can be used to quickly create more ATP. When the muscle starts to contract and needs energy, creatine phosphate transfers its phosphate back to ADP to form ATP and creatine. This reaction is catalyzed by the enzyme creatine kinase"
- **C** — _reversed_relationship_: ADP is a product of ATP hydrolysis, not the fuel; it is released after the power stroke rather than split to provide energy, so it cannot be the molecule the myosin head hydrolyzes to drive cycling.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 17439-17445: "ATP is converted to ADP and Pi by the intrinsic ATPase activity of myosin... After the power stroke, ADP is released; however, the formed cross-bridge is still in place"
- **D** — _adjacent_fact_: GTP is a high-energy nucleotide produced in the Krebs cycle and used in other cellular processes, but the myosin head's intrinsic ATPase specifically hydrolyzes ATP—not GTP—to power the cross-bridge cycle.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, line 50509: "For each turn of the cycle, three NADH, one ATP (through GTP), and one FADH2 are created"; contrasted with lines 17439-17440: "ATP is converted to ADP and Pi by the intrinsic ATPase activity of myosin"

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Organ Systems.' The answer is A because the intrinsic ATPase activity of the myosin head hydrolyzes \(\text{ATP}\) to \(\text{ADP}\) and \(\text{P}_\text{i}\), and the energy released re-cocks the head for the next cross-bridge cycle. The textbook states that after \(\text{ATP}\) binding causes the myosin head to detach from actin, "\(\text{ATP}\) is converted to ADP and Pi by the intrinsic ATPase activity of myosin" and "The energy released during ATP hydrolysis changes the angle of the myosin head into a cocked position" (openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 17439-17441). It further notes that the repetitive pulling of the myosin heads "requires energy, which is provided by ATP" (lines 17409-17411) and that "As long as ATP is available, it readily attaches to myosin, the cross-bridge cycle can recur, and muscle contraction can continue" (lines 17446-17447). (Choice A) \(\text{ATP}\) is correct: it is the molecule that binds the myosin head, is split by myosin's own ATPase enzyme, and supplies the energy that drives every attach-pull-detach-recock cycle. (Choice B) Creatine phosphate is wrong because it is only an energy reserve that "transfers its phosphate back to ADP to form ATP" via creatine kinase (lines 17467-17469); it regenerates \(\text{ATP}\) but is never the molecule hydrolyzed by the myosin head itself. (Choice C) \(\text{ADP}\) is wrong because it is a product of \(\text{ATP}\) hydrolysis, not the fuel; it is released after the power stroke (line 17445), not split to release energy. (Choice D) \(\text{GTP}\) is wrong because, although it is a high-energy nucleotide used elsewhere (e.g., one \(\text{GTP}\)/\(\text{ATP}\) is generated per turn of the Krebs cycle, line 50509), myosin's ATPase hydrolyzes \(\text{ATP}\), not \(\text{GTP}\), to power cross-bridge cycling. This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall that myosin's intrinsic ATPase hydrolyzes \(\text{ATP}\) to power repeated cross-bridge cycling.

## Q6 — Cross-Bridge Cycle & Sliding Filament · **easy** (topic easy/struct easy) · Skill 1 · 75s · conf 5
**Stem:** A motor neuron stops firing, but acetylcholine (ACh) molecules from the previous action potential still occupy receptors on the motor end-plate. Which event most directly allows the sarcolemma to repolarize and the muscle fiber to relax?

- **A.** Voltage-gated \(\text{Na}^+\) channels open at the end-plate
- **B.** \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) is pumped from the sarcoplasm back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum

**Correct (D) citation:** openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt lines 17288-17291: "Immediately following depolarization of the membrane, it repolarizes, re-establishing the negative membrane potential. Meanwhile, the ACh in the synaptic cleft is degraded by the enzyme acetylcholinesterase (AChE) so that the ACh cannot rebind to a receptor and reopen its channel, which would cause unwanted extended muscle excitation and contraction."

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _reversed_relationship_: Opening voltage-gated sodium channels causes depolarization and propagation of the action potential, the opposite of repolarization; it would extend stimulation rather than end it.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt lines 17283-17285: "another set of ion channels called voltage-gated sodium channels are triggered to open. Sodium ions enter the muscle fiber, and an action potential rapidly spreads (or “fires”) along the entire membrane to initiate excitation-contraction coupling."
- **B** — _adjacent_fact_: Pumping Ca2+ back into the SR is a genuine relaxation step but occurs inside the fiber after repolarization and does not clear ACh from the cleft or repolarize the sarcolemma.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt lines 17538-17540: "The muscle fiber will repolarize, which closes the gates in the SR where Ca++ was being released. ATP-driven pumps will move Ca++ out of the sarcoplasm back into the SR."
- **C** — _process_step_confusion_: Tropomyosin reshielding actin-binding sites halts cross-bridge formation on the thin filament downstream of falling Ca2+; it does not remove ACh from the cleft or repolarize the membrane.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt lines 17540-17541: "This results in the “reshielding” of the actin-binding sites on the thin filaments. Without the ability to form cross-bridges between the thin and thick filaments, the muscle fiber loses its tension and relaxes."

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Organ Systems.' The answer is D because the enzyme acetylcholinesterase (AChE) breaks down ACh in the synaptic cleft so the neurotransmitter cannot rebind its receptor and reopen the channel, ending stimulation and permitting the membrane to repolarize. The textbook states that the ACh in the synaptic cleft is degraded by the enzyme acetylcholinesterase (AChE) so that the ACh cannot rebind to a receptor and reopen its channel, which would cause unwanted extended muscle excitation and contraction. As long as ACh remains bound, its receptor channel stays open and positive ions keep entering, holding the membrane depolarized; clearing the ACh is therefore the most direct event that lets repolarization re-establish the negative membrane potential. (Choice A) Voltage-gated \(\text{Na}^+\) channels opening drives depolarization and propagation of the action potential, the opposite of repolarization, so this event would prolong rather than end stimulation. (Choice B) Pumping \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum is a real relaxation step, but it acts inside the fiber on the contractile machinery and follows repolarization; it does not clear ACh from the cleft or repolarize the sarcolemma. (Choice C) Tropomyosin reshielding actin-binding sites stops cross-bridge cycling and is a downstream consequence of falling \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\); it occurs on the thin filament and does not affect the ACh occupying end-plate receptors. (Choice D) Degradation of ACh by acetylcholinesterase removes the agonist from the cleft, closing the receptor channels so the sarcolemma can repolarize, which is the most direct trigger described. This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall that acetylcholinesterase degrades ACh in the synaptic cleft to terminate end-plate stimulation and permit repolarization.
