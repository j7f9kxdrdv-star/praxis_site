## Q1 — Muscle Fiber Types · **easy** (topic easy/struct easy) · Skill 1 · 75s · conf 5
**Stem:** A histologist examines a postural muscle and notes that its slow oxidative fibers appear deep red under the microscope. This red coloration most directly reflects an abundance of a molecule that supports the fibers' sustained, fatigue-resistant contractions by performing which function?

- **A.** Storing \(\text{O}_2\) within the fiber for aerobic ATP production ✅
- **B.** Storing glycogen for rapid anaerobic glycolysis
- **C.** Housing electron transport for oxidative phosphorylation
- **D.** Carrying \(\text{O}_2\) through the bloodstream in red blood cells

**Correct (A):** openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 17826-17829: "The SO fibers also possess myoglobin, an O2-carrying molecule similar to O2-carrying hemoglobin in the red blood cells. The myoglobin stores some of the needed O2 within the fibers themselves (and gives SO fibers their red color). All of these features allow SO fibers to produce large quantities of ATP, which can sustain muscle activity without fatiguing for long periods of time."

**Distractors:**
- **B** — _adjacent_fact_: Glycogen is a real muscle fuel, but it is the stored substrate for anaerobic glycolysis characteristic of white FG fibers, not the molecule that gives SO fibers their red color or supports their aerobic ATP production.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 17845-17848: "FG fibers primarily use anaerobic glycolysis as their ATP source. They have a large diameter and possess high amounts of glycogen, which is used in glycolysis to generate ATP quickly to produce high levels of tension. Because they do not primarily use aerobic metabolism, they do not possess substantial numbers of mitochondria or significant amounts of myoglobin and therefore have a white color."
- **C** — _misconception_: SO fibers genuinely contain many mitochondria that perform oxidative phosphorylation, but the textbook attributes the red color specifically to myoglobin's stored oxygen, not to the mitochondria; mitochondria are an associated oxidative feature, not the pigment.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 17822-17827: "uses oxygen (O2) in the metabolic pathway, occurs in the mitochondria. The SO fibers possess a large number of mitochondria... The SO fibers also possess myoglobin, an O2-carrying molecule... The myoglobin stores some of the needed O2 within the fibers themselves (and gives SO fibers their red color)."
- **D** — _reversed_relationship_: Hemoglobin carries oxygen in red blood cells within the bloodstream, but the molecule giving the fiber itself its red color is the intracellular myoglobin that stores oxygen inside the fiber; the answer reverses the intracellular store with the circulating carrier.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, line 17826: "The SO fibers also possess myoglobin, an O2-carrying molecule similar to O2-carrying hemoglobin in the red blood cells. The myoglobin stores some of the needed O2 within the fibers themselves."

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Organ Systems.' The answer is A because the red color of slow oxidative fibers comes from myoglobin, an \(\text{O}_2\)-carrying molecule that stores oxygen inside the fiber to fuel aerobic ATP production. The textbook states that SO fibers "also possess myoglobin, an \(\text{O}_2\)-carrying molecule similar to \(\text{O}_2\)-carrying hemoglobin in the red blood cells. The myoglobin stores some of the needed \(\text{O}_2\) within the fibers themselves (and gives SO fibers their red color)" (lines 17826-17827), and elsewhere that "Myoglobin is found in the sarcoplasm and acts as an oxygen storage supply for the mitochondria" (lines 17872-17873). This stored oxygen, combined with abundant mitochondria and capillaries, lets SO fibers "produce large quantities of ATP, which can sustain muscle activity without fatiguing for long periods of time" (lines 17828-17829), which is why they dominate fatigue-resistant postural muscles. (Choice A) is correct: myoglobin holds an intracellular oxygen reserve that feeds aerobic respiration, and its iron-bound oxygen is precisely what makes the fiber red. (Choice B) is wrong because glycogen, not myoglobin, is the stored fuel for anaerobic glycolysis and is a hallmark of white FG fibers that "possess high amounts of glycogen, which is used in glycolysis" (lines 17845-17846); glycogen does not produce the red color. (Choice C) is wrong because, although SO fibers do contain many mitochondria that carry out oxidative phosphorylation (line 17822), the textbook attributes the red color specifically to myoglobin, not to the mitochondria themselves. (Choice D) is wrong because hemoglobin carries \(\text{O}_2\) in red blood cells within the bloodstream, whereas the molecule responsible for the fiber's own red color is the intracellular myoglobin stored inside the fiber (line 17826); the question asks about the intracellular molecule, not the circulating one. This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall the specific function of myoglobin and why it makes slow oxidative fibers red.

- **C** — _adjacent_fact_: Fast oxidative fibers are fatigue-resistant but are matched to walking-type movements requiring more energy than postural control, and they fatigue more quickly than SO fibers because they may switch to anaerobic glycolysis; postural muscles contain relatively few FO fibers.

(Choice C) is wrong: fast oxidative fibers are fatigue-resistant but are used for movements such as walking that require more energy than postural control, and because they may switch to anaerobic glycolysis they fatigue more quickly than SO fibers. (Choice D) is wrong: cardiac muscle fibers are found only in the heart wall, not in skeletal postural muscles, so they are not a type of skeletal fiber that holds the spine upright. This is a Scientific Reasoning and Problem Solving question because you must apply the metabolic and fatigue properties of each fiber type to a specific functional demand.

## Q4 — Muscle Fiber Types · **medium** (topic medium/struct medium) · Skill 1 · 105s · conf 5
**Stem:** A histologist comparing two skeletal muscle fibers finds that one is densely packed with mitochondria while the other contains very few. The mitochondria-rich fiber is most directly suited to which capacity?

- **A.** Sustaining ATP production through aerobic metabolism ✅
- **B.** Generating ATP rapidly through anaerobic glycolysis
- **C.** Producing high tension through faster cross-bridge cycling
- **D.** Storing reserve \(\text{O}_2\) within the sarcoplasm itself

The SO fibers possess a large number of mitochondria and are capable of contracting for longer periods because of the large amount of ATP they can produce... All of these features allow SO fibers to produce large quantities of ATP, which can sustain muscle activity without fatiguing for long periods of time."

**Distractors:**
- **B** — _reversed_relationship_: Anaerobic glycolysis is the hallmark pathway of fast glycolytic fibers, which the text says "do not possess substantial numbers of mitochondria"; moreover glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm, so abundant mitochondria do not support anaerobic glycolysis.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 17845-17847: "FG fibers primarily use anaerobic glycolysis as their ATP source... Because they do not primarily use aerobic metabolism, they do not possess substantial numbers of mitochondria or significant amounts of myoglobin."
- **C** — _adjacent_fact_: Contraction speed and rapid cross-bridge cycling depend on the rate of myosin ATPase ATP hydrolysis (fast fibers being roughly twice as fast), not on mitochondrial density, and SO fibers explicitly do not produce a large amount of tension, so mitochondrial abundance does not confer faster cross-bridge cycling or high tension.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 17809-17811: "The speed of contraction is dependent on how quickly myosin's ATPase hydrolyzes ATP to produce cross-bridge action. Fast fibers hydrolyze ATP approximately twice as quickly as slow fibers, resulting in much quicker cross-bridge cycling."
- **D** — _process_step_confusion_: Intracellular oxygen storage is performed by myoglobin in the sarcoplasm, not by mitochondria; mitochondria consume O2 during aerobic metabolism rather than serving as an O2 reserve.  
  ↳ openstax-anatomy-physiology-2e.txt, lines 17826-17827, 17872-17873: "The SO fibers also possess myoglobin, an O2-carrying molecule... The myoglobin stores some of the needed O2 within the fibers themselves"; "Myoglobin is found in the sarcoplasm and acts as an oxygen storage supply for the mitochondria."

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Organ Systems.' The answer is A because aerobic metabolism occurs inside mitochondria, so a fiber packed with mitochondria is built to make ATP aerobically for sustained, fatigue-resistant contraction. The textbook states that oxidative fibers contain many more mitochondria than glycolytic fibers "because aerobic metabolism, which uses oxygen (O2) in the metabolic pathway, occurs in the mitochondria" (lines 17821-17822). Slow oxidative fibers possess a large number of mitochondria and can contract for longer periods because of the large amount of ATP they produce aerobically, allowing them to sustain activity without fatiguing (lines 17822-17829). This is exactly why abundant mitochondria mark a fiber for endurance rather than for brief, powerful bursts. (Choice A) is correct: the high mitochondrial density reflects reliance on oxidative (aerobic) ATP production, which yields more ATP per cycle and resists fatigue (lines 17813-17819, 17821-17822). (Choice B) reverses the relationship, because anaerobic glycolysis is the pathway of fast glycolytic fibers, which "do not possess substantial numbers of mitochondria"; glycolysis proceeds in the cytoplasm, not the mitochondria (lines 17845-17847). (Choice C) names an adjacent property that is unrelated to mitochondria, since contraction speed and rapid cross-bridge cycling depend on how quickly myosin ATPase hydrolyzes ATP, with fast fibers hydrolyzing ATP about twice as quickly as slow fibers (lines 17809-17811). (Choice D) confuses organelles, because intracellular \(\text{O}_2\) storage is the role of myoglobin held in the sarcoplasm, not of mitochondria, which consume \(\text{O}_2\) rather than store it (lines 17826-17827, 17872-17873). This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall that mitochondria are the site of aerobic (oxidative) ATP production and link their abundance to sustained, fatigue-resistant contraction.
