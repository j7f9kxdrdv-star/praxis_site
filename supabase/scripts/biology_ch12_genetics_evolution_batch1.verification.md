## Q1 — Mendelian Foundations · **easy** · Skill 1 · 70s · conf 5
**Stem:** A geneticist examines a pair of homologous chromosomes in a diploid cell and notes that the gene for seed color sits at the identical band on both members of the pair. The fixed chromosomal address occupied by that gene is most accurately termed its

- **A.** locus ✅
- **B.** allele
- **C.** genotype
- **D.** chromatid

**Correct (A):** openstax-biology-2e.txt line 13298 (glossary): "locus position of a gene on a chromosome"; and lines 12414-12415: "Homologous chromosomes are the same length and have specific nucleotide segments called genes in exactly the same location, or locus."

**Distractors:**
- **B** — _adjacent_fact_: An allele is one alternative version of a gene that occupies the locus, not the physical position itself. The text distinguishes the two: at matching loci the genes on the two homologs "are not identical" because they may be different alleles, so an allele is the variant residing at the site rather than the site.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 12435-12436: "the different genes (alleles) themselves are not identical, although they code for the same traits such as 'hair color.'"
- **C** — _misconception_: A genotype is the underlying genetic makeup (the combination of alleles an organism carries for a gene), describing the genetic content, not the chromosomal position the gene occupies.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt line 15597 (glossary): "genotype underlying genetic makeup, consisting of [alleles]."
- **D** — _scale_unit_error_: A chromatid is an entire single DNA molecule (a duplicated copy of the whole chromosome) held at the centromere, a structural unit far larger than the position of one gene; it is the wrong scale for naming a single gene's address.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 13292-13293 (glossary): "chromatid single DNA molecule of two strands of duplicated DNA and associated proteins held together at the centromere."

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Transmission of Heritable Information from Generation to Generation.' The answer is A because the locus is the fixed physical position that a gene occupies on a chromosome. The OpenStax glossary defines locus as the "position of a gene on a chromosome," and the text states that homologous chromosomes "have specific nucleotide segments called genes in exactly the same location, or locus." Because homologous chromosomes are matched pairs that carry the same genes in the same order, the seed-color gene maps to the same locus on each homolog, even though the two copies may carry different alleles. (Choice A) is correct: the locus is the chromosomal address of the gene, and corresponding genes line up at identical loci on the two homologs. (Choice B) names an allele, which is one alternative version of the gene that resides at the locus, not the position itself; the text notes that the genes at matching loci on the two homologs "are not identical" because they may be different alleles, so an allele is the variant occupying the site rather than the site. (Choice C) names a genotype, which is the combination of alleles an organism carries for a gene, again describing the genetic content rather than the physical position on the chromosome. (Choice D) names a chromatid, which is one of the two duplicated DNA strands of a single chromosome held together at the centromere, an entire structural copy of the chromosome rather than the specific spot where one gene sits. This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall the definition of a locus and how genes are positioned on homologous chromosomes.

## Q2 — Mendelian Foundations · **easy** · Skill 1 · 75s · conf 5
**Stem:** In a pea population where the allele for violet flowers (\(A\)) is completely dominant over the allele for white flowers (\(a\)), a researcher grows two violet-flowered plants that appear identical. Which finding is most consistent with Mendelian inheritance in this dominant–recessive system?

- **A.** Both plants must carry the genotype \(aa\)
- **B.** Both plants must carry the genotype \(Aa\)
- **C.** The plants must share an identical genotype
- **D.** One plant may be \(AA\) while the other is \(Aa\) ✅

**Correct (D):** openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 14740-14742: "For a gene that is expressed in a dominant and recessive pattern, homozygous dominant and heterozygous organisms will look identical (that is, they will have different genotypes but the same phenotype). The traits of the recessive allele will only be observed in homozygous recessive individuals." Also lines 14717-14719: "The observable traits expressed by an organism are referred to as its phenotype. An organism's underlying genetic makeup, consisting of both physically visible and non-expressed alleles, is called its genotype."

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _misconception_: The genotype \(aa\) is homozygous recessive and produces the recessive (white) phenotype, not the observed violet flowers; OpenStax states the recessive trait is only seen in homozygous recessive individuals.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 14741-14742: "The traits of the recessive allele will only be observed in homozygous recessive individuals."
- **B** — _partial_truth_: Although \(Aa\) does yield violet flowers, it is not the only genotype that does so, so the plants are not required to be heterozygous; a violet plant may instead be homozygous dominant \(AA\).  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 14740-14741: "homozygous dominant and heterozygous organisms will look identical (that is, they will have different genotypes but the same phenotype)."
- **C** — _reversed_relationship_: This reverses the genotype-phenotype relationship by assuming identical phenotype forces identical genotype; OpenStax shows organisms can have different genotypes but the same phenotype.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 14740-14741: "homozygous dominant and heterozygous organisms will look identical (that is, they will have different genotypes but the same phenotype)."

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Transmission of Heritable Information from Generation to Generation.' The answer is D because in a dominant–recessive system the violet phenotype is produced by both the homozygous dominant and the heterozygous genotype, so two violet plants can carry different genotypes. The phenotype is the observable trait, whereas the genotype is the organism's underlying allele combination, and OpenStax states that for a gene expressed in a dominant and recessive pattern, "homozygous dominant and heterozygous organisms will look identical (that is, they will have different genotypes but the same phenotype)." Because a single dominant \(A\) allele is sufficient to mask the recessive \(a\) allele, both \(AA\) and \(Aa\) plants display violet flowers, and only the homozygous recessive \(aa\) genotype yields the white phenotype. Identical appearance therefore does not require identical genotype. (Choice A) is incorrect because \(aa\) is the homozygous recessive genotype, which would produce white flowers, not the observed violet phenotype. (Choice B) is incorrect because, although \(Aa\) does give violet flowers, it is not the only genotype that does so; a violet plant could equally be \(AA\), so neither plant is required to be heterozygous. (Choice C) is incorrect because it reverses the genotype–phenotype relationship by assuming matching phenotypes guarantee matching genotypes, whereas different genotypes (\(AA\) and \(Aa\)) can yield the same phenotype. (Choice D) is correct: a dominant allele needs only one copy to be expressed, so the two phenotypically identical violet plants may legitimately differ, with one homozygous dominant (\(AA\)) and the other heterozygous (\(Aa\)). This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall the distinction between genotype and phenotype and that a dominant trait arises from more than one genotype.

## Q3 — Mendelian Foundations · **easy** · Skill 1 · 70s · conf 5
**Stem:** A pea plant inherits one allele for violet flowers (\(V\)) and one allele for white flowers (\(v\)), where violet is fully dominant. The plant produces violet flowers. Regarding the white-flower allele in this heterozygous plant, which outcome is most consistent with Mendel's law of dominance?

- **A.** It produces flowers of an intermediate, pale-violet color
- **B.** It remains latent and is not expressed in the phenotype ✅
- **C.** It is expressed simultaneously alongside the violet allele
- **D.** It is the only allele transmitted to the gametes

**Correct (B):** openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 15204-15208: \"Mendel's law of dominance states that in a heterozygote, one trait will conceal the presence of another trait for the same characteristic. Rather than both alleles contributing to a phenotype, the dominant allele will be expressed exclusively. The recessive allele will remain 'latent' but will be transmitted to offspring by the same manner in which the dominant allele is transmitted.\" Also lines 14740-14742: \"homozygous dominant and heterozygous organisms will look identical... The traits of the recessive allele will only be observed in homozygous recessive individuals.\"

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _adjacent_fact_: An intermediate, blended phenotype in the heterozygote is incomplete dominance, an exception to simple dominance, not what happens when violet is fully dominant over white. Under full dominance the heterozygote looks identical to the homozygous dominant.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 14906-14908: 'This pattern of inheritance is described as incomplete dominance, denoting the expression of two contrasting alleles such that the individual displays an intermediate phenotype.'
- **C** — _adjacent_fact_: Simultaneous expression of both alleles in the heterozygote is codominance, not simple dominant/recessive inheritance; in full dominance only the dominant allele's trait appears.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 14924-14926: 'A variation on incomplete dominance is codominance, in which both alleles for the same characteristic are simultaneously expressed in the heterozygote.'
- **D** — _reversed_relationship_: The recessive allele is masked in the phenotype but is still transmitted to gametes by the same mechanism as the dominant allele; the dominant allele is not the sole allele passed on. This reverses the masking of the phenotype into a (false) claim about transmission.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 15205-15207: 'the dominant allele will be expressed exclusively. The recessive allele will remain "latent" but will be transmitted to offspring by the same manner in which the dominant allele is transmitted.'

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Transmission of Heritable Information from Generation to Generation.' The answer is B because under Mendel's law of dominance the dominant allele is expressed exclusively in a heterozygote, so the recessive white-flower allele stays latent and makes no contribution to the phenotype. Mendel's law of dominance states that in a heterozygote, one trait will conceal the presence of another trait for the same characteristic, with the dominant allele expressed exclusively while the recessive allele remains \"latent\" but is still transmitted to offspring. Because of this, a homozygous dominant (\(VV\)) plant and a heterozygous (\(Vv\)) plant look identical, and the recessive trait is observed only in homozygous recessive (\(vv\)) individuals. (Choice A) The intermediate, blended phenotype describes incomplete dominance, in which the heterozygote displays a phenotype between the two parents (as in a pink \(C^R C^W\) snapdragon); this is an exception to simple dominance, not what occurs when violet is fully dominant. (Choice B) is correct: under full dominance the recessive allele is masked and contributes nothing to the phenotype, though it is still passed on intact to the next generation. (Choice C) Simultaneous expression of both alleles in the heterozygote defines codominance (as in the MN blood groups), not the simple dominant/recessive pattern described here. (Choice D) The recessive allele is transmitted to gametes by the same manner as the dominant allele, so the dominant allele is not the only one passed on; this reverses the fact that segregation gives each gamete one allele of the pair. This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall that, in a heterozygote, a fully dominant allele is expressed exclusively while the recessive allele remains latent.

## Q4 — Mendelian Foundations · **medium** · Skill 2 · 105s · conf 5
**Stem:** A couple in which neither parent shows cystic fibrosis, a recessive disorder, has a child who expresses the disease. The child most likely exhibits the recessive phenotype while the parents do not because the child is the only one of the three who:

- **A.** carries a single copy of the recessive allele
- **B.** inherited the recessive allele only from the mother
- **C.** carries two copies of the recessive allele ✅
- **D.** expresses the dominant allele exclusively

**Correct (C):** openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 14740-14742: "For a gene that is expressed in a dominant and recessive pattern, homozygous dominant and heterozygous organisms will look identical... The traits of the recessive allele will only be observed in homozygous recessive individuals." Reinforced at lines 15207-15208: "The recessive trait will only be expressed by offspring that have two copies of this allele... and these offspring will breed true when self-crossed."

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _misconception_: A single copy of the recessive allele is precisely the heterozygous carrier state of each unaffected parent; one copy does not produce the recessive phenotype because the dominant allele is expressed exclusively. This choice describes why the parents are unaffected, not why the child is affected.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 14893-14894: "in the presence of the dominant allele, the recessive allele is hidden and makes no contribution to the phenotype. Therefore, recessive alleles can be 'carried' and not expressed by individuals. Such heterozygous individuals are sometimes referred to as 'carriers.'"
- **B** — _partial_truth_: Inheriting the recessive allele from only the mother would make the child a heterozygous carrier, not affected; expression of a recessive trait requires two copies, meaning one recessive allele from each parent, since the allele is autosomal rather than X-linked.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 15207-15208: "The recessive trait will only be expressed by offspring that have two copies of this allele." Also lines 15221-15224: each parent's paired factors "segregate equally into gametes," so an affected homozygote must receive a recessive allele from both parents.
- **D** — _reversed_relationship_: Exclusive expression of the dominant allele is what makes the heterozygous parents (and any unaffected individual) display the normal phenotype; it is the opposite of the affected child, who expresses the recessive trait precisely because no dominant allele is present to mask it.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 15204-15206: "Mendel's law of dominance states that in a heterozygote, one trait will conceal the presence of another... the dominant allele will be expressed exclusively."

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Transmission of Heritable Information from Generation to Generation.' The answer is C because the recessive cystic fibrosis phenotype appears only when an individual is homozygous recessive, carrying two copies of the recessive allele. The textbook states that for a gene expressed in a dominant and recessive pattern, "The traits of the recessive allele will only be observed in homozygous recessive individuals," and that "The recessive trait will only be expressed by offspring that have two copies of this allele." Both unaffected parents are heterozygous carriers, each holding one recessive allele that remains "latent" and makes no contribution to the phenotype because the dominant allele is expressed exclusively; only their child, who received a recessive allele from each parent, is homozygous recessive \(aa\) and therefore expresses the disease. (Choice A) is incorrect: a single copy of the recessive allele describes the heterozygous carrier state of each parent, in which the recessive allele is hidden and not expressed, so it cannot explain why the child is affected. (Choice B) is incorrect: a recessive phenotype requires a recessive allele from BOTH parents, not just the mother; cystic fibrosis is autosomal, so inheriting the allele from only one parent would leave the child as a heterozygous carrier rather than affected. (Choice C) is correct: only the child is homozygous recessive, having inherited one recessive allele from each carrier parent, which is the two-copy requirement for expressing a recessive trait; the parents each carry only one copy and so remain unaffected. (Choice D) is incorrect: exclusive expression of the dominant allele describes the unaffected parents (and any unaffected child), in whom the dominant allele masks the recessive one, which is the opposite of the affected child's situation. This is a Scientific Reasoning and Problem Solving question because you must apply the principle that a recessive phenotype requires two copies of the recessive allele to a specific pedigree situation and identify the child's unique homozygous recessive genotype.

## Q5 — Mendelian Foundations · **medium** · Skill 2 · 100s · conf 5
**Stem:** A geneticist records the single-locus genotypes of four diploid individuals as \(VV\), \(vv\), \(L^M L^M\), and \(I^A I^B\). Which individual is heterozygous at that locus?

- **A.** The \(VV\) individual
- **B.** The \(vv\) individual
- **C.** The \(L^M L^M\) individual
- **D.** The \(I^A I^B\) individual ✅

**Correct (D):** openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 14730-14732: "all of the offspring were heterozygous for the contrasting trait, meaning that their genotype reflected that they had different alleles for the gene being examined." Also lines 14728-14729: "organisms that are homozygous at a given gene, or locus, have two identical alleles for that gene on their homologous chromosomes."

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _misconception_: \(VV\) is two identical dominant alleles, i.e., homozygous dominant, not heterozygous; the trap is assuming uppercase/dominant alleles signal a heterozygote. Heterozygous is defined by the two alleles being different, and the two \(V\) alleles are identical.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 14777-14778: "we would refer to the genotype of a homozygous dominant pea plant with violet flowers as VV"
- **B** — _adjacent_fact_: \(vv\) is two identical recessive alleles, i.e., homozygous recessive; recessive expression requires homozygosity, but homozygous recessive is not heterozygous. The two \(v\) alleles are identical, so this is homozygous.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt line 14778: "a homozygous recessive pea plant with white flowers as vv"
- **C** — _misconception_: \(L^M L^M\) carries two identical \(L^M\) alleles, making it homozygous; the superscript notation used in codominant systems can be mistaken for two different alleles, but identical superscripts mean identical alleles. Only \(L^M L^N\) (different superscripts) would be heterozygous.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt line 14928: "Homozygotes (LMLM and LNLN) express either the M or the N allele, and heterozygotes (LMLN) express both alleles equally."

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Transmission of Heritable Information from Generation to Generation.' The answer is D because the \(I^A I^B\) individual carries two different alleles at the locus, which is the definition of heterozygous. A diploid organism is heterozygous when its two alleles for a gene differ from each other; the textbook states that when parents with contrasting traits were crossed, the offspring were 'heterozygous for the contrasting trait, meaning that their genotype reflected that they had different alleles for the gene being examined.' By contrast, an organism is homozygous when it has 'two identical alleles for that gene on their homologous chromosomes.' Classification depends only on whether the two alleles are identical or different, not on whether the letters are uppercase, lowercase, or carry superscripts. (Choice A) is incorrect because \(VV\) consists of two identical dominant alleles, making it homozygous dominant; the textbook designates \(VV\) as the genotype of 'a homozygous dominant pea plant.' (Choice B) is incorrect because \(vv\) consists of two identical recessive alleles and is therefore homozygous recessive, designated for 'a homozygous recessive pea plant.' (Choice C) is incorrect because \(L^M L^M\) carries two copies of the same \(L^M\) allele; the textbook lists genotypes such as \(L^M L^M\) among 'Homozygotes,' so two matching superscripted alleles are still homozygous. (Choice D) is correct: \(I^A I^B\) pairs two distinct alleles (\(I^A\) and \(I^B\)) at one locus, satisfying the definition of having 'different alleles for the gene being examined'; both alleles happening to be dominant or codominant does not change the fact that the two alleles are non-identical, so the individual is heterozygous. This is a Scientific Reasoning and Problem Solving question because you must apply the definitions of homozygous and heterozygous to classify specific genotype strings rather than simply restate the definitions.

## Q6 — Mendelian Foundations · **medium** · Skill 2 · 105s · conf 5

The expression of the trait in this hemizygous son is most directly explained by which feature of his genotype?

- **A.** He carries only one allele for the gene, with none to mask it ✅
- **B.** The recessive allele behaves dominantly in male offspring

- **D.** X inactivation silenced his wild-type allele

**Distractors:**
- **B** — _reversed_relationship_: The recessive allele does not become dominant in males. The textbook states that for XY males, hemizygosity "makes the descriptions of dominance and recessiveness irrelevant" (line 15044) — the allele is expressed because it is the only one present, not because it has acquired dominance.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 15043-15044: "Hemizygosity makes the descriptions of dominance and recessiveness irrelevant for XY males."
- **C** — _process_step_confusion_: This applies the female requirement to a male. The textbook says "Females must inherit recessive X-linked alleles from both of their parents in order to express the trait" (lines 15104-15105), whereas a hemizygous XY male has only one X and thus only one allele — not two copies.

Whatever single allele he inherits is therefore expressed directly, which is why "human males need to inherit only one recessive mutant X allele to be affected." (Choice A) is correct: with a single X-linked allele and no homologous partner on the Y, the recessive allele is the only one present and is expressed regardless of its dominance classification. (Choice B) misstates the mechanism as a reversed relationship: the recessive allele does not become dominant in males; rather, dominance and recessiveness are simply irrelevant because there is no second allele for it to act against. (Choice C) confuses the male situation with the female one: the textbook notes that "Females must inherit recessive X-linked alleles from both of their parents in order to express the trait" (an \(X^h X^h\) genotype), but a hemizygous \(X^h Y\) son has only one X and therefore only one copy of the allele, not two. (Choice D) names a real but misapplied process: X inactivation can cause carrier females (genotype \(X^H X^h\)) to manifest mild forms of a trait by silencing the dominant wild-type allele on one X, but a hemizygous male has no second X to inactivate and no wild-type allele to silence. This is a Scientific Reasoning and Problem Solving question because you must apply the principle of hemizygosity to a specific inheritance scenario to identify why a single recessive X-linked allele is expressed.

## Q8 — Mendelian Foundations · **medium** · Skill 1 · 105s · conf 5
**Stem:** A woman whose sibling has cystic fibrosis, an autosomal recessive disorder, has no symptoms of the disease but is found to carry one copy of the recessive allele. Which of the following statements about this woman is/are accurate?

I. She expresses a phenotype intermediate between affected and unaffected individuals.
II. She is homozygous recessive at the cystic fibrosis locus.
III. She can transmit the recessive allele to her children.

- **A.** I only
- **B.** III only ✅
- **C.** I and III only
- **D.** II and III only

**Correct (B):** openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 14893-14896: \"in the presence of the dominant allele, the recessive allele is hidden and makes no contribution to the phenotype. Therefore, recessive alleles can be 'carried' and not expressed by individuals. Such heterozygous individuals are sometimes referred to as 'carriers.'\" Also lines 14739-14742: \"homozygous dominant and heterozygous organisms will look identical (that is, they will have different genotypes but the same phenotype). The traits of the recessive allele will only be observed in homozygous recessive individuals.\"

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _misconception_: Statement I is false: a simple recessive carrier looks identical to a homozygous dominant individual, not intermediate. An intermediate phenotype is the hallmark of incomplete dominance, a different inheritance pattern, not of a heterozygous carrier of a recessive disorder.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt line 14908: "This pattern of inheritance is described as incomplete dominance, denoting the expression of two contrasting alleles such that the individual displays an intermediate phenotype." Contrast with lines 14739-14742: "homozygous dominant and heterozygous organisms will look identical."
- **C** — _partial_truth_: This pairs the true statement III with the false statement I. Statement III is correct, but accepting statement I wrongly assumes the carrier shows an intermediate phenotype; a heterozygote in simple dominant/recessive inheritance is phenotypically identical to a homozygous dominant individual.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 14739-14742: "homozygous dominant and heterozygous organisms will look identical (that is, they will have different genotypes but the same phenotype)."
- **D** — _adjacent_fact_: This pairs the true statement III with the false statement II. A carrier is heterozygous (\(Aa\)), not homozygous recessive (\(aa\)); a homozygous recessive individual would actually have cystic fibrosis, since recessive traits are observed only in homozygous recessive individuals.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 14741-14742: "The traits of the recessive allele will only be observed in homozygous recessive individuals." Also lines 14893-14894 describing carriers as "heterozygous individuals."

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Transmission of Heritable Information from Generation to Generation.' The answer is B because the woman is a heterozygous carrier (\(Aa\)) who is phenotypically unaffected yet still carries one recessive allele that she can pass to a gamete and thus to her children. A heterozygous individual who carries a recessive allele but does not express it is referred to as a carrier; the recessive allele is \"carried\" and not expressed because, in the presence of the dominant allele, the recessive allele is hidden and makes no contribution to the phenotype. ## Q23 — Mendel's Laws & Epigenetics · **hard** · Skill 2 · 150s · conf 5
**Stem:** A pea plant heterozygous for seed color and seed shape, with the two genes on separate chromosomes, produces four kinds of gametes—\(YR\), \(Yr\), \(yR\), and \(yr\)—in equal proportions, so that the non-parental allele combinations \(Yr\) and \(yR\) appear just as often as the parental combinations. This equal representation of every allele combination across the two genes is most directly attributable to which principle?

- **A.** Equal segregation of paired alleles
- **B.** Independent assortment of the two genes ✅
- **C.** Dominance of one allele over another
- **D.** Linkage of genes on a chromosome

**Correct (B):** openstax-biology-2e.txt, lines 15234-15235: "Mendel's law of independent assortment states that genes do not influence each other with regard to the sorting of alleles into gametes, and every possible combination of alleles for every gene is equally likely to occur." Thus, there are four equally likely gametes that can be formed when the YyRr heterozygote is self-crossed, as follows: YR, Yr, yR, and yr."

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _partial_truth_: Equal segregation is genuinely operating in this cross—each gamete receives exactly one allele of each gene—but the law of segregation addresses how the two alleles of a SINGLE gene separate, not how alleles of two different genes combine. It cannot, on its own, explain why the non-parental combinations Yr and yR occur as often as the parental ones; that equal mixing across genes requires independent assortment.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt, lines 15221-15222: 'This law states that paired unit factors (genes) must segregate equally into gametes such that offspring have an equal likelihood of inheriting either factor.' This describes one gene's two alleles separating, not combinations across genes; lines 15266-15267 separate the two roles: 'the law of segregation requires that each gamete receive either an R allele or an r allele along with either a Y allele or a y allele. The law of independent assortment states that a gamete into which an r allele sorted would be equally likely to contain either a Y allele or a y allele.'
- **C** — _adjacent_fact_: Dominance describes which allele is phenotypically expressed in a heterozygote for the same characteristic; it does not determine the frequencies with which allele combinations are sorted into gametes. The scenario concerns gamete composition, not phenotypic expression, so dominance is irrelevant to the equal four-way ratio.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt, lines 15204-15206: 'Mendel's law of dominance states that in a heterozygote, one trait will conceal the presence of another trait for the same characteristic. Rather than both alleles contributing to a phenotype, the dominant allele will be expressed exclusively.'
- **D** — _reversed_relationship_: Linkage produces the opposite of what is observed: genes physically close on the same chromosome tend to be inherited together, so parental combinations would predominate and the non-parental Yr and yR gametes would be underrepresented. The equal frequency of all four gamete classes indicates the genes are NOT linked but assort independently.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt, lines 15384-15386: 'The segregation of alleles into gametes can be influenced by linkage, in which genes that are located physically close to each other on the same chromosome are more likely to be inherited as a pair.' Line 15383 adds that 'Genes that are located on separate non-homologous chromosomes will always sort independently,' matching the scenario's separate-chromosome stipulation.

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Transmission of Heritable Information from Generation to Generation.' The answer is B because the equal appearance of every combination of alleles across two different genes—including the non-parental \(Yr\) and \(yR\) gametes—is the defining signature of independent assortment. Mendel's law of independent assortment states that genes do not influence each other with regard to the sorting of alleles into gametes, and every possible combination of alleles for every gene is equally likely to occur. Because the color gene and the shape gene sort without influencing each other, a gamete that received the \(y\) allele is equally likely to carry \(R\) or \(r\), yielding all four gamete classes \(YR\), \(Yr\), \(yR\), and \(yr\) in equal proportion. The question asks which principle governs combinations across different genes, and that is precisely what independent assortment describes. (Choice A) Equal segregation explains why a single heterozygous gene contributes one allele of that gene to each gamete with equal likelihood, but by itself it does not predict how alleles of two different genes combine; segregation alone would still leave open whether the genes mix freely or stay together, so it cannot account for the equal frequency of the non-parental combinations. (Choice B) is correct: independent assortment specifies that every combination of alleles across the two genes is equally likely, which is exactly the equal four-way gamete ratio described. (Choice C) Dominance governs which of two alleles for the same characteristic is expressed in the phenotype of a heterozygote; it says nothing about the frequencies with which allele combinations are packaged into gametes. (Choice D) Linkage would produce the opposite outcome—genes located close together on the same chromosome tend to be transmitted together, so the parental combinations would predominate and the non-parental \(Yr\) and \(yR\) gametes would be underrepresented rather than equally frequent. This is a Scientific Reasoning and Problem Solving question because you must apply Mendel's laws to a described gamete distribution and identify which principle, rather than the meiotic mechanism, accounts for the equal mixing of allele combinations across two genes.
