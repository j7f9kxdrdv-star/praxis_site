## Q1 — Natural Selection & Fitness · **easy** · Skill 1 · 75s · conf 5
**Stem:** In a population of marine fish, a single female releases hundreds of thousands of eggs each spawning season, yet the habitat can sustain only a small fraction of the resulting young to maturity. Within Darwin's framework of natural selection, this overproduction of offspring relative to available resources directly establishes which condition in each generation?

- **A.** Random change in allele frequencies
- **B.** Competition among individuals for limited resources ✅
- **C.** Generation of new alleles by mutation
- **D.** Measurement of fitness relative to the population

**Correct (B):** openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 21694-21698: "Second, more offspring are produced than are able to survive, so resources for survival and reproduction are limited. The capacity for reproduction in all organisms outstrips the availability of resources to support their numbers. Thus, there is competition for those resources in each generation."

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _misconception_: Random change in allele frequencies is genetic drift, a non-selective process driven by chance rather than by overproduction and resource competition; the student conflates the random mechanism with Darwin's resource-driven tenet.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 22956-22958: "Another way a population's allele and genotype frequencies can change is genetic drift...which is simply the effect of chance. By chance, some individuals will have more offspring than others—not due to an advantage conferred by some genetically-encoded trait."
- **C** — _adjacent_fact_: Mutation is a true and adjacent fact about evolution (the ultimate source of new alleles), but it explains the origin of variation that selection acts on, not the condition that overproduction relative to resources establishes.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 21767-21768: "Mutation, a change in DNA, is the ultimate source of new alleles, or new genetic variation in any population."
- **D** — _adjacent_fact_: Relative fitness is a real and related concept, but it is a downstream measurement comparing an individual's reproductive contribution to the rest of the population, not the competitive condition that overproduction directly creates.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 23158-23160: "it is not an individual's absolute fitness that counts, but rather how it compares to the other organisms in the population. Scientists call this concept relative fitness."

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Transmission of Heritable Information from Generation to Generation.' The answer is B because Darwin reasoned that more offspring are produced than can survive, so resources for survival and reproduction are limited; as the textbook states, 'The capacity for reproduction in all organisms outstrips the availability of resources to support their numbers. Thus, there is competition for those resources in each generation' (lines 21694-21698). Overproduction relative to resources is the tenet that sets up this struggle, and only the individuals with inherited variations that best compete will survive and have more offspring (lines 21699-21701). (Choice A) Random change in allele frequencies describes genetic drift, which the textbook defines as 'simply the effect of chance...not due to an advantage conferred by some genetically-encoded trait' (lines 22956-22958), a non-selective process distinct from the resource competition overproduction creates. (Choice B) is correct: overproduction beyond what resources can support produces competition for those limited resources in each generation. (Choice C) Generation of new alleles by mutation is the ultimate source of new genetic variation (lines 21767-21768), but it is the origin of the variation selection acts on, not the condition that overproduction establishes. (Choice D) Relative fitness is how an individual's reproductive contribution compares 'to the other organisms in the population' (lines 23158-23160), a downstream measure of selection's outcome rather than the condition overproduction directly creates. This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall Darwin's tenet that the overproduction of offspring relative to limited resources produces competition within each generation.

## Q2 — Natural Selection & Fitness · **medium** · Skill 2 · 105s · conf 5
**Stem:** A hospital lab treats a large bacterial culture with an antibiotic, and over successive generations the proportion of resistant cells rises sharply. Regarding the resistance allele responsible, the most defensible account of its origin is that it

- **A.** was induced by exposure to the antibiotic
- **B.** spread by chance rather than by survival
- **C.** was already present at low frequency ✅
- **D.** was newly created by directional selection

**Correct (C):** OpenStax Biology 2e, lines 21996-22005: "It is also important to understand that the variation that natural selection works on is already in a population and does not arise in response to an environmental change... The gene for resistance was already present in the bacteria's gene pool, likely at a low frequency... Experiments have demonstrated that mutations for antibiotic resistance do not arise as a result of antibiotic application."

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _misconception_: This is the Lamarckian induced-variation error: the textbook explicitly states resistance "did not arise by mutation because of applying the antibiotic" and that "mutations for antibiotic resistance do not arise as a result of antibiotic application." The antibiotic selects, it does not induce the allele.  
  ↳ OpenStax Biology 2e, lines 22001-22005: "The resistance, which a gene causes, did not arise by mutation because of applying the antibiotic... Experiments have demonstrated that mutations for antibiotic resistance do not arise as a result of antibiotic application."
- **B** — _adjacent_fact_: Spreading "by chance rather than by survival" defines genetic drift, which is "simply the effect of chance" and "not due to an advantage conferred by some genetically-encoded trait." Here the resistant cells rise specifically because they survive the antibiotic, which is selection (differential survival), not random drift.  
  ↳ OpenStax Biology 2e, lines 22956-22959: "genetic drift... which is simply the effect of chance. By chance, some individuals will have more offspring than others—not due to an advantage conferred by some genetically-encoded trait..."
- **D** — _reversed_relationship_: This reverses the source of novelty: directional selection only shifts the frequency of existing phenotypes when the environment changes; it cannot create new alleles. The textbook states natural selection "can only... select—it cannot create," and attributes novel traits to mutation.  
  ↳ OpenStax Biology 2e, lines 22707-22709: "natural selection can only, as its name implies, select—it cannot create. We can attribute novel traits and behaviors to another evolutionary force—mutation."

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Transmission of Heritable Information from Generation to Generation.' The answer is C because the variation that natural selection acts on is already present in a population and does not arise in response to an environmental change; the textbook states that applying antibiotics selects for resistant bacteria, but "the gene for resistance was already present in the bacteria's gene pool, likely at a low frequency," and that "mutations for antibiotic resistance do not arise as a result of antibiotic application" (OpenStax Biology 2e, lines 21996-22005). The antibiotic does not generate the allele; it merely kills non-resistant cells, so the pre-existing resistant minority survives and divides, raising the allele's frequency. This illustrates that natural selection can only "select—it cannot create" novel variation (line 22708). (Choice A) is incorrect: the resistance "did not arise by mutation because of applying the antibiotic," so exposure does not induce the allele—this is the Lamarckian induced-variation misconception the passage explicitly refutes (lines 22001-22005). (Choice B) is incorrect: spreading "by chance rather than by survival" describes genetic drift, "simply the effect of chance" and "not due to an advantage conferred by some genetically-encoded trait" (lines 22956-22959); here the increase is driven by differential survival of resistant cells, which is selection, not drift. (Choice C) is correct: the allele "was already present in the bacteria's gene pool, likely at a low frequency" before exposure (lines 22001-22002). (Choice D) is incorrect: directional selection shifts phenotype frequencies when the environment changes (line 23178), but selection "cannot create" new alleles—mutation, not selection, is the source of novel variation (lines 22707-22709). This is a Scientific Reasoning and Problem Solving question because you must apply the principle that selection acts on pre-existing variation to a described antibiotic-resistance scenario and rule out induced-mutation and chance-based accounts.

## Q5 — Natural Selection & Fitness · **hard** · Skill 2 · 150s · conf 5
**Stem:** In a beetle population exposed to an insecticide, an allele conferring insecticide resistance sits so close on the same chromosome to a recessive lethal allele that crossover almost never separates them. Despite the resistance allele's clear survival advantage against the insecticide, its frequency falls over successive generations. This decline is best explained by the principle that natural selection acts on:

- **A.** rare alleles faster than common alleles
- **B.** the whole organism's net fitness, not isolated alleles ✅
- **C.** phenotypes through random sampling of gametes
- **D.** alleles entering a population through migration

**Correct (B):** Biology 2e, lines 23151-23156: "Natural selection acts on entire organisms, not on an individual allele within the organism. An individual may carry a very beneficial genotype with a resulting phenotype that, for example, increases the ability to reproduce (fecundity), but if that same individual also carries an allele that results in a fatal childhood disease, that fecundity phenotype will not pass to the next generation because the individual will not live to reach reproductive age. Natural selection acts at the individual's level. It selects for individuals with greater contributions to the gene pool of the next generation. Scientists call this an organism's evolutionary (Darwinian) fitness." Supported by lines 15385-15386: "gametes can be influenced by linkage, in which genes that are located physically close to each other on the same chromosome."

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _misconception_: Selection does not act on alleles faster simply because they are rare; OpenStax explains that a rare recessive deleterious allele persists because mating of two carriers 'will not happen frequently enough for natural selection to be able to swiftly eliminate the allele' (lines 22935-22937) — rarity slows, not speeds, removal, and selection still works through the whole organism, not allele rarity.  
  ↳ Biology 2e, lines 22935-22937: 'it will not happen frequently enough for natural selection to be able to swiftly eliminate the allele from the population, and as a result, the allele maintains itself at low levels in the gene pool.'
- **C** — _adjacent_fact_: Random sampling of gametes/chance describes genetic drift, a NON-selective force, not natural selection; OpenStax defines genetic drift as the 'effect of chance on a population's gene pool' (lines 23389-23390), whereas the scenario's allele loss is driven by fitness-based selection acting on the linked lethal allele, not by chance.  
  ↳ Biology 2e, lines 23389-23390: 'genetic drift effect of chance on a population's gene pool.'
- **D** — _adjacent_fact_: Alleles entering a population through migration is gene flow, an independent evolutionary force; OpenStax defines gene flow as the 'flow of alleles in and out of a population due to the individual or gamete migration' (lines 23385-23386), which is not the mechanism causing a linked beneficial allele to be lost within this closed population.  
  ↳ Biology 2e, lines 23385-23386: 'gene flow flow of alleles in and out of a population due to the individual or gamete migration.'

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Transmission of Heritable Information from Generation to Generation.' The answer is B because natural selection screens individuals by their overall (net) Darwinian fitness, not by any single allele in isolation. OpenStax states that "Natural selection acts on entire organisms, not on an individual allele within the organism. An individual may carry a very beneficial genotype... but if that same individual also carries an allele that results in a fatal childhood disease, that... phenotype will not pass to the next generation because the individual will not live to reach reproductive age" (Biology 2e, lines 23151-23156). Because the beneficial resistance allele is tightly linked to a recessive lethal allele and crossover rarely separates them (linkage occurs when "genes... are located physically close to each other on the same chromosome," lines 15385-15386), individuals carrying the resistance allele also tend to carry the lethal allele, lowering their net fitness and dragging the beneficial allele down with it. (Choice A) is incorrect: selection does not preferentially target alleles by rarity; rarity slows the removal of recessive deleterious alleles ("it will not happen frequently enough for natural selection to... swiftly eliminate the allele," lines 22935-22937), the opposite of "faster." (Choice B) is correct: selection operates on the integrated organism's relative reproductive success, so a beneficial allele linked to a lethal one can be lost (lines 23151-23156). (Choice C) is incorrect: random sampling of gametes describes genetic drift, the "effect of chance on a population's gene pool" (lines 23389-23390), a non-selective force, not the fitness-based mechanism here. (Choice D) is incorrect: alleles entering through migration describe gene flow, the "flow of alleles in and out of a population due to... migration" (lines 23385-23386), an unrelated force not operating in this closed scenario. This is a Scientific Reasoning and Problem Solving question because you must apply the principle that selection acts on whole-organism net fitness to a described linkage scenario to explain why a beneficial allele is lost.

## Q7 — Natural Selection & Fitness · **medium** · Skill 2 · 105s · conf 5

The most direct reason natural selection fails to drive height evolution here is that the height variation

- **A** — _misconception_: The scenario explicitly states tall plants reliably produce more seeds, so the trait is advantageous; the barrier to evolution is the lack of heritability, not a lack of fitness benefit.   ↳ Biology 2e, lines 21762-21763: "Natural selection can only take place if there is variation... these differences must have some genetic basis; otherwise, the selection will not lead to change in the next generation."
- **B** — _process_step_confusion_: Confuses the source of variation with the requirement for selection. OpenStax states that for selection to act, differences among individuals "must have some genetic basis; otherwise, the selection will not lead to change in the next generation," and explicitly gives the example of "an individual's height because of better nutrition rather than different genes" (Biology 2e, lines 21762-21765). (Choice B) is incorrect: variation that selection works on "is already in a population and does not arise in response to an environmental change," as with antibiotic-resistance alleles that predate the antibiotic (lines 21998-22005); even if soil-driven height did arise in response to soil, that misstates the actual barrier, which is the absence of a genetic basis. (Choice C) is correct: only heritable, genetically based variation can respond to selection, and nutrition-driven height is the textbook's own example of variation lacking that basis (lines 21762-21765, 22906-22911). (Choice D) is incorrect: small population size magnifies random genetic drift, a different and non-selective force, rather than explaining why selection cannot act on a non-genetic trait (lines 22807-22811); population size is not the issue described. This is a Scientific Reasoning and Problem Solving question because you must apply the principle that selection requires heritable variation to a described scenario and reason from the soil-driven, non-genetic cause of the variation to the conclusion that the population cannot evolve.

## Q8 — Natural Selection & Fitness · **hard** · Skill 2 · 150s · conf 5
**Stem:** A population of bark beetles currently shows a rising frequency of a dark (melanic) allele because soot-darkened trunks let predatory birds easily pick off the lighter beetles. A student concludes that the lineage is steadily progressing toward a permanently superior, optimally camouflaged form. This reasoning is flawed because natural selection in this population can only do which of the following?

- **A.** Generate new camouflage alleles when needed
- **B.** Increase the population's absolute fitness ceiling
- **C.** Act directly on individual advantageous alleles

**Correct (D):** openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 22007-22013: "In a larger sense, evolution is not goal directed. Species do not become 'better' over time. Thus, it is limited by a population's existing genetic variability and whatever new alleles arise through mutation and gene flow."

**Distractors:**
- **A** — _misconception_: Selection cannot generate alleles on demand: OpenStax says it 'does not create anything from scratch' and is 'limited by a population's existing genetic variability' (lines 23318-23320), and that mutations for a trait 'do not arise as a result of' the selective pressure (lines 22000-22005). New variation comes from mutation and gene flow, not from the environmental need itself.  
  ↳ openstax-biology-2e.txt lines 23318-23320: "Natural selection can only select on existing variation in the population. It does not create anything from scratch. Thus, it is limited by a population's existing genetic variability and whatever new alleles arise through mutation and gene flow." Lines 22004-22005: "mutations for antibiotic resistance do not arise as a result of antibiotic application."
