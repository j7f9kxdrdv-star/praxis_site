## Q1 — Endomembrane System & Organelle Identification — hard (topic:medium/struct:hard) — Skill 2 — ~150s — conf 5 (g3)
**Stem:** A pancreatic acinar cell synthesizes digestive enzymes that are released into the duct lumen as glycoproteins. A researcher attaches a fluorescent tag to one such enzyme the instant its polypeptide first acquires a carbohydrate group, then tracks the tagged molecule until it leaves the cell. Where does the enzyme MOST likely first acquire that carbohydrate, and what is its final step before release?

- A. It first acquires the carbohydrate in the rough endoplasmic reticulum lumen, and is released when a secretory vesicle from the Golgi fuses with the plasma membrane. ✅
- B. It first acquires the carbohydrate in the Golgi apparatus, and is released when a secretory vesicle from the Golgi fuses with the plasma membrane.
- C. It first acquires the carbohydrate in the rough endoplasmic reticulum lumen, and is released when a lysosome fuses with the plasma membrane.
- D. It first acquires the carbohydrate on a cytoplasmic ribosome, and is released when a secretory vesicle from the Golgi fuses with the plasma membrane.

**Correct A** — Lines 5664-5667: "Membrane and secretory proteins are synthesized in the rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER)... a (green) integral membrane protein is modified by attachment of a (purple) carbohydrate in the ER. Vesicles... bud from the ER and fuse with the Golgi apparatus' cis face. As the protein passes along the Golgi's cisternae, the addition of more carbohydrates further modifies it." Lines 5771-5773: "the modified and tagged proteins are packaged into secretory vesicles that bud from the Golgi's trans face... other secretory vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane and release their contents outside the cell."
**Distractors:** B=partial_truth (Golgi-glycosylation-true-but-not-first: correct that the Golgi adds sugars, wrong that it is the initial glycosylation site); C=misconception (lysosome-as-secretory-vesicle: confusing the degradative organelle with the exocytic secretory vesicle); D=process_step_confusion (ribosome-as-modification-site: attributing co-translational lumenal modification to the ribosome itself)
**Distractor citations:**
  - Choice B (Golgi as first glycosylation site) is wrong per lines 5666-5667: "As the protein passes along the Golgi's cisternae, the addition of MORE carbohydrates further modifies it"—the Golgi adds additional sugar to a chain that began in the ER, so it is not the FIRST site.
  - Choice C (lysosome fuses with plasma membrane to release the enzyme) is wrong per lines 5547-5557: "The lysosomes are the cell's 'garbage disposal.' ... Enzymes within the lysosomes aid in breaking down proteins, polysaccharides, lipids, nucleic acids, and even worn-out organelles"—lysosomes degrade material at acidic pH; the secretory/exocytic step uses Golgi-derived secretory vesicles (lines 5771-5773).
  - Choice D (carbohydrate acquired on a cytoplasmic ribosome) is wrong per lines 5699-5702: "Ribosomes transfer their newly synthesized proteins into the RER's lumen where they undergo structural modifications, such as folding or acquiring side chains"—modification occurs after transfer into the RER lumen, not on the ribosome itself.
**Hard-tier self-check:** Meets 3 of the 4 hard-tier conditions. (1) Concept-chaining: 3 linked reasoning steps—ribosome on RER -> co-translational entry into the RER lumen where carbohydrate is FIRST attached -> Golgi adds more carbohydrate/sorts -> secretory vesicle exocytosis. The item requires resolving TWO bundled variables at once (first glycosylation site AND terminal release step). (3) True partial-truth trap: Choice B is correct under a different framing—the Golgi genuinely IS a glycosylation site (\"adding short sugar molecule chains\")—and is only wrong because the stem specifies the FIRST carbohydrate, which is added in the ER. A student with partial knowledge who recalls 'Golgi = sugar modification' is punished. (4) 510-tempting distractor: Choice B is the answer a strong student reaches after one subtle error (conflating 'where most sugar processing happens' with 'where glycosylation begins'). Wrong paths punished: B = Golgi-as-initial-glycosylation (ignores ER N-linked initiation); C = lysosome-as-exocytic-vesicle (confuses degradation with secretion); D = ribosome-as-modification-site (ignores lumenal transfer requirement). A hard topic alone did not earn the tag—the structural traps do.

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Cell and Tissue Structure (BIO)'. The answer is A because carbohydrate attachment to a secreted glycoprotein first occurs in the rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER), and the molecule's final step is exocytosis when a Golgi-derived secretory vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane. As a ribosome on the RER translates the enzyme, the polypeptide enters the RER lumen, where it is modified—including the initial attachment of a carbohydrate (OpenStax 4.4, Figure 4.18, lines 5664-5667: \"a (green) integral membrane protein is modified by attachment of a (purple) carbohydrate in the ER\"; lines 5699-5702: \"Ribosomes transfer their newly synthesized proteins into the RER's lumen where they undergo structural modifications... The proteins can also secrete from the cell\"). The protein then travels by transport vesicle to the Golgi cis face, where \"the addition of more carbohydrates further modifies it\" (lines 5666-5667) and it is sorted and tagged. Finally it is \"packaged into secretory vesicles that bud from the Golgi's trans face\" which \"fuse with the plasma membrane and release their contents outside the cell\" (lines 5771-5773). (Choice A) Correct: the carbohydrate is added in the ER lumen first and the Golgi later adds more, so the FIRST attachment site is the RER; exocytosis of a Golgi-derived secretory vesicle is indeed the terminal step for a secreted enzyme. (Choice B) Partial-truth trap: the Golgi genuinely glycosylates proteins—\"the most frequent modification is adding short sugar molecule chains\" (lines 5766-5767)—but it adds MORE carbohydrate to a chain that began in the ER (\"the addition of more carbohydrates further modifies it,\" lines 5666-5667); because the stem asks where the carbohydrate is FIRST acquired, the Golgi is the site of subsequent, not initial, glycosylation. (Choice C) Reversed/incorrect destination: lysosomes are the cell's \"garbage disposal\" whose acidic hydrolytic enzymes break down macromolecules and worn-out organelles (lines 5547-5557); they degrade intracellular material rather than fusing with the plasma membrane to release a functional secreted enzyme, so this misroutes the exocytic step. (Choice D) Process-step confusion: free cytoplasmic ribosomes synthesize the polypeptide, but glycosylation does not occur on the ribosome itself—the protein must first be transferred into the RER lumen, where modifications such as carbohydrate attachment take place (lines 5699-5702), so the carbohydrate cannot be acquired on a cytoplasmic ribosome. This is a Scientific Reasoning and Problem Solving question because you must integrate the sequence of endomembrane events and apply it to predict where carbohydrate is first added and what the terminal secretory step is.

## Q2 — Endomembrane System & Organelle Identification — hard (topic:medium/struct:hard) — Skill 4 — ~150s — conf 5 (g6)
**Stem:** A researcher disrupts liver cells and separates the homogenate by differential centrifugation into three fractions, then assays each for three marker enzymes. Acid phosphatase marks lysosomes, cytochrome c oxidase marks mitochondria, and catalase marks peroxisomes. Relative activity (units per mg protein) is shown below. \(\begin{array}{lccc} \text{Marker enzyme} & \text{Fraction I} & \text{Fraction II} & \text{Fraction III} \\ \text{Acid phosphatase} & 8 & 5 & 71 \\ \text{Cytochrome c oxidase} & 64 & 9 & 6 \\ \text{Catalase} & 7 & 73 & 10 \end{array}\) The organelle that most likely predominates in Fraction II is the:

- A. peroxisome, because its catalase detoxifies \(\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\) generated during oxidation reactions ✅
- B. mitochondrion, because its cytochrome c oxidase consumes \(\text{O}_2\) during oxidation reactions
- C. lysosome, because its acid hydrolases degrade macromolecules at low pH
- D. chloroplast, because its catalase supports the oxidative reactions of metabolism

**Correct A** — Lines 5507-5511: "Peroxisomes are small, round organelles enclosed by single membranes. They carry out oxidation reactions that break down fatty acids and amino acids... Many of these oxidation reactions release hydrogen peroxide, H2O2, which would be damaging to cells; however, when these reactions are confined to peroxisomes, enzymes safely break down the H2O2 into oxygen and water." Catalase is the confined enzyme, so its peak in a fraction marks peroxisome enrichment.
**Distractors:** B=partial_truth (correct-under-a-different-fraction: right marker-organelle pairing applied to the wrong fraction by anchoring on 'oxidation uses oxygen'); C=process_step_confusion (valid marker-organelle pairing assigned to the wrong centrifugation fraction); D=misconception (wrong-cell-type organelle (plant chloroplast) invoked for an animal liver sample with a misattributed enzyme)
**Distractor citations:**
  - Choice B (mitochondrion), lines 5485-5488: "Mitochondria are oval-shaped, double membrane organelles... The inner layer has folds called cristae... The cristae and the matrix have different roles in cellular respiration." Cytochrome c oxidase is the inner-membrane respiratory-chain marker; per the table it peaks in Fraction I (64), not Fraction II, so the mitochondrion does not predominate in Fraction II.
  - Choice C (lysosome), lines 5548-5556: "Enzymes within the lysosomes aid in breaking down proteins, polysaccharides, lipids, nucleic acids... These enzymes are active at a much lower pH than the cytoplasm's. Therefore, the pH within lysosomes is more acidic." Acid phosphatase is this low-pH hydrolase marker; per the table it peaks in Fraction III (71), so lysosomes predominate in Fraction III, not Fraction II.
  - Choice D (chloroplast), lines 5594-5604: "The chloroplasts contain a green pigment, chlorophyll, which captures the light energy that drives the reactions of photosynthesis. Like plant cells, photosynthetic protists also have chloroplasts." Chloroplasts are plant/protist plastids absent from liver cells and are sites of photosynthesis, not catalase-based detoxification, so they cannot be the Fraction II organelle.
**Hard-tier self-check:** Meets 3 of the 4 hard-tier gate conditions. (1) Data-to-mechanism reasoning: the student must convert a marker-enzyme activity profile into an organelle identity (catalase -> peroxisome), not read a value. (2) Concept-chaining, 3 linked steps: (a) recall which enzyme marks which organelle, (b) locate the fraction where the relevant marker peaks, (c) tie peroxisomal catalase to its biological role of detoxifying H2O2 from oxidation reactions. (3) True partial-truth trap: Choice B is correct under a different fraction — cytochrome c oxidase genuinely is the mitochondrial marker and mitochondria genuinely run oxygen-using oxidation, so the pairing is real but peaks in Fraction I, punishing a reader who anchors on 'oxidation needs oxygen.' Wrong paths punished: B = anchoring on oxygen/oxidation and skipping the catalase-to-peroxisome mapping; C = correct lysosome marker but reading the wrong column (Fraction III); D = importing a plant-only organelle into an animal liver sample. Choice B is also the 510-tempting distractor reached after a single subtle error.

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Cell and Tissue Structure (BIO)'. The answer is A because catalase is the peroxisomal marker enzyme, and Fraction II has by far the highest catalase activity, so it is enriched in peroxisomes. Each membrane-bound organelle carries signature enzymes, so the fraction in which a marker enzyme is most concentrated is the fraction enriched in that marker's organelle. The textbook states that peroxisomes "carry out oxidation reactions that break down fatty acids and amino acids" and that "many of these oxidation reactions release hydrogen peroxide, \(\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\) ... when these reactions are confined to peroxisomes, enzymes safely break down the \(\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\) into oxygen and water" (lines 5508-5511). Catalase is that confined enzyme, so its peak in Fraction II identifies the peroxisome; the data must be read as marker-to-organelle, not as the absolute size of any one number. (Choice A) is correct: catalase tracks the peroxisome, Fraction II's catalase activity (73) dwarfs its activity elsewhere, and the peroxisome is the organelle that detoxifies the \(\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\) byproduct of its own oxidation reactions, consistent with lines 5508-5511. (Choice B) is a partial-truth trap: cytochrome c oxidase does mark mitochondria and mitochondria do use \(\text{O}_2\) in oxidation reactions on the inner membrane (lines 5485-5488), but cytochrome c oxidase peaks in Fraction I (64), not Fraction II, so a reader who anchors on "oxidation reactions use oxygen, therefore mitochondria" reaches the wrong fraction. (Choice C) is wrong because acid phosphatase, the lysosomal acid-hydrolase marker active "at a much lower pH than the cytoplasm's" (lines 5548-5556), is highest in Fraction III (71), so lysosomes predominate there, not in Fraction II. (Choice D) is wrong because chloroplasts are plastids found only in plant cells, not in the liver tissue assayed, and they perform photosynthesis rather than serving as the home of catalase (lines 5594-5604); attributing the catalase signal to a chloroplast misassigns both the enzyme and the cell type. This is a Data-based and Statistical Reasoning question because you must compare marker-enzyme activities across centrifugation fractions and infer organelle identity from the enzyme that is most enriched, rather than reading a single value off the table.

## Q3 — Endomembrane System & Organelle Identification — medium (topic:medium/struct:medium) — Skill 1 — ~105s — conf 5 (g8)

Which of the listed structures is the screen MOST likely to flag as positive? (I) Mitochondria; (II) Nucleus; (III) Rough endoplasmic reticulum; (IV) Peroxisomes.

- A. I only ✅
- B. I and II
- C. I and IV
- D. II and III

**Correct A** — Line 5485: "Mitochondria are oval-shaped, double membrane organelles ... that have their own ribosomes and DNA." Lines 5611-5626 (Endosymbiosis): "both mitochondria and chloroplasts contain DNA and ribosomes ... bacteria have DNA and ribosomes, just like mitochondria and chloroplasts ... the aerobic bacteria becoming mitochondria."
**Distractors:** B=partial_truth (Nucleus holds the cell's DNA, so a student treats the nuclear genome as a separate organellar genome rather than the cell's central linear-chromosome DNA); C=misconception (Treats peroxisomes as semi-autonomous DNA-containing organelles like mitochondria because both are oval/round metabolic organelles); D=adjacent_fact (Confuses endomembrane organelles (rough ER) and the nucleus with DNA-bearing self-replicating organelles)
**Distractor citations:**
  - Choice B (nucleus): Line 5398 "The nucleus ... houses the cell's DNA"; lines 5425-5428 "chromosomes, structures within the nucleus that are made up of DNA, the hereditary material ... In eukaryotes, chromosomes are linear structures" — this is the nuclear genome itself, not a separate organellar genome.
  - Choice C (peroxisomes): Lines 5507-5508 "Peroxisomes are small, round organelles enclosed by single membranes. They carry out oxidation reactions that break down fatty acids and amino acids." No DNA mentioned; lines 5611 & 5622-5627 name only mitochondria and chloroplasts as having their own DNA.
  - Choice D (rough ER + nucleus): Lines 5648-5650 "The endomembrane system ... includes the nuclear envelope, lysosomes, and vesicles ... and the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus" — the rough ER is an endomembrane organelle that processes proteins and has no genome; line 5653 confirms the endomembrane system excludes mitochondria.

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Cell and Tissue Structure (BIO)'. The answer is A because, in an animal cell, the mitochondrion is the only listed compartment that carries its own DNA and the ribosomal machinery to replicate it independently of the nucleus. The textbook states that mitochondria "have their own ribosomes and DNA" (line 5485), and the endosymbiosis passage explains why: mitochondria descend from ingested aerobic bacteria, so they retain "DNA and ribosomes, just like" bacteria (lines 5611-5626). Among the four listed structures, only the mitochondrion fits the screen for a circular organellar genome plus self-replication machinery; chloroplasts share this trait but are absent from animal cells, leaving mitochondria as the sole positive. (Choice A) Correct. Only structure I (mitochondria) carries a genome separate from the nuclear DNA and the means to copy it; the endosymbiotic origin from a free-living bacterium accounts for both the circular DNA and the organelle's own ribosomes (lines 5485, 5611-5626). In an animal cell, chloroplasts (the other organelle with its own genome) are not present, so the set is correctly I only. (Choice B) The nucleus (II) houses the cell's chromatin and "the cell's DNA" (lines 5398, 5425-5428), but that is the nuclear genome itself organized into linear chromosomes, not a separate organellar circular genome; the screen seeks DNA distinct from the nuclear genome, so the nucleus is excluded by definition. (Choice C) Peroxisomes (IV) are "small, round organelles enclosed by single membranes" that carry out oxidation reactions (lines 5507-5508); they contain no DNA and do not appear in the endosymbiosis account, which names only mitochondria and chloroplasts as having their own genome (lines 5611, 5622-5627). (Choice D) The rough endoplasmic reticulum (III) is a component of the endomembrane system that modifies and transports proteins (lines 5648-5650); it is genome-free, and pairing it with the nucleus (II) doubles the error. This is a Knowledge of Scientific Concepts and Principles question because you must recall which eukaryotic organelles retain their own genome and self-replication machinery as a consequence of endosymbiotic origin.

## Q4 — Endomembrane System & Organelle Identification — medium (topic:medium/struct:medium) — Skill 4 — ~90s — conf 5 (g24)
**Stem:** A fibroblast actively secreting collagen is given a brief pulse of radiolabeled amino acids, and the labeled procollagen recovered from three compartments is measured at three successive chase times: \( \begin{array}{cccc} \text{chase time} & \text{ER} & \text{Golgi} & \text{secretory vesicles} \\ 3\ \text{min} & 82\% & 14\% & 4\% \\ 20\ \text{min} & 18\% & 55\% & 27\% \\ 60\ \text{min} & 5\% & 21\% & 74\% \end{array} \) Based on the shifting distribution of label across the compartments, which trafficking route does the procollagen most likely follow?

- A. It is made in secretory vesicles and moves backward through the Golgi to the ER.
- B. It is synthesized first in the Golgi, then split between the ER and secretory vesicles.
- C. It is synthesized in the ER and moves sequentially to the Golgi and then to secretory vesicles. ✅
- D. It enters the ER, Golgi, and secretory vesicles at the same time from a shared cytosolic pool.

**Correct C** — Lines 5664-5668: "Membrane and secretory proteins are synthesized in the rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER). ... Vesicles with the integral protein bud from the ER and fuse with the Golgi apparatus' cis face. As the protein passes along the Golgi's cisternae, the addition of more carbohydrates further modifies it. After its synthesis is complete, it exits as an integral membrane protein of the vesicle that buds from the Golgi's trans face."
**Distractors:** A=reversed_relationship (Pathway direction reversed (vesicles to Golgi to ER)); B=process_step_confusion (Synthesis misassigned to the Golgi instead of the ER); D=misconception (Parallel loading from a shared cytosolic pool rather than sequential vesicular transport)
**Distractor citations:**
  - Line 5771 (refutes A): "Finally, the modified and tagged proteins are packaged into secretory vesicles that bud from the Golgi's trans face." — vesicle formation is the LAST step, not the first, so the reverse order is impossible.
  - Lines 5699-5701 (refutes B): "Ribosomes transfer their newly synthesized proteins into the RER's lumen where they undergo structural modifications... The proteins can also secrete from the cell" — synthesis occurs in the RER, not the Golgi.
  - Lines 5764-5766 (refutes D): "The transport vesicles that formed from the ER travel to the cis face, fuse with it, and empty their contents into the Golgi apparatus' lumen." — movement between compartments is by sequential vesicular transport, not simultaneous loading from the cytosol.

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Cell and Tissue Structure (BIO)'. The answer is C because the label appears first and most heavily in the ER, then peaks in the Golgi at the intermediate time, and finally accumulates in secretory vesicles, tracing a one-way ER \( \to \) Golgi \( \to \) vesicle route. The text states that "Membrane and secretory proteins are synthesized in the rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER)" and that "Vesicles with the integral protein bud from the ER and fuse with the Golgi apparatus' cis face," after which the protein "exits as an integral membrane protein of the vesicle that buds from the Golgi's trans face." The data match this directional flow: at 3 min the label is \( 82\% \) in the ER (the site of synthesis), by 20 min it has shifted to the Golgi (\( 55\% \)), and by 60 min it is \( 74\% \) in secretory vesicles. Because the peak simply moves from one compartment to the next over time rather than appearing everywhere at once or in reverse, the procollagen must travel ER first, Golgi second, vesicles last. (Choice A) The reverse order is contradicted by the data, since the earliest time point shows label concentrated in the ER, not in secretory vesicles; the text places synthesis in the RER and budding from the Golgi's trans face last. (Choice B) Synthesis does not begin in the Golgi; the text specifies that secretory proteins "are synthesized in the rough endoplasmic reticulum," consistent with the label appearing in the ER first. (Choice C) Correct: the label moves ER \( \to \) Golgi \( \to \) secretory vesicles in sequence, matching the text's statement that vesicles bud from the ER, fuse with the Golgi cis face, and later bud from the trans face. (Choice D) Simultaneous entry from a cytosolic pool is ruled out because the compartments do not all label at once; the label shifts compartment by compartment, and the text describes vesicular transport between organelles rather than direct cytosolic loading of each one. This is a Data-based and Statistical Reasoning question because you must infer the direction of a transport pathway from how the percentage of label redistributes among compartments across successive time points.

## Q5 — Endomembrane System & Organelle Identification — hard (topic:medium/struct:hard) — Skill 4 — ~150s — conf 5 (g25)
**Stem:** A plant biologist isolates two double-membrane organelles—one a mitochondrion, one a chloroplast—from a single leaf cell; both organelles carry their own circular DNA and ribosomes. She then exposes both isolated organelles to a compound that completely and selectively inactivates cytochrome \(c\) oxidase. Assuming each organelle is supplied with its normal substrates and the chloroplast is illuminated, which outcome most directly follows?

- A. The chloroplast halts ATP-coupled sugar synthesis while the mitochondrion is unaffected, because cytochrome \(c\) oxidase is the terminal enzyme of the chloroplast's light reactions
- B. Both organelles continue normally, because cytochrome \(c\) oxidase acts in the cytosol during glycolysis rather than inside either double-membrane organelle
- C. The mitochondrion's ATP output drops but continues at a reduced rate, because its matrix citric-acid-cycle enzymes are untouched and can still drive inner-membrane ATP synthesis on their own
- D. The mitochondrion's inner-membrane ATP synthesis stops while the illuminated chloroplast continues photosynthesis, because cytochrome \(c\) oxidase is a respiratory electron-transport component absent from the chloroplast ✅

**Correct D** — Line 9618: "Cyanide inhibits cytochrome c oxidase, a component of the electron transport chain." and Line 5495: "ATP synthesis takes place on the inner membrane." plus Lines 5574-5575: "Like the mitochondria, chloroplasts have their own DNA and ribosomes, but chloroplasts have an entirely different function. Chloroplasts are plant cell organelles that carry out photosynthesis." — establishes that cytochrome c oxidase is a mitochondrial respiratory ETC component whose loss stops mitochondrial inner-membrane ATP synthesis, while the chloroplast's separate photosynthetic function is unaffected (Choice D).
**Distractors:** A=reversed_relationship (Assigns the respiratory enzyme cytochrome c oxidase to the chloroplast's light reactions, swapping which endosymbiont-derived organelle is affected); B=process_step_confusion (Misplaces cytochrome c oxidase into cytosolic glycolysis instead of the mitochondrial inner-membrane ETC); C=partial_truth (Correctly notes the citric-acid-cycle matrix enzymes are untouched but wrongly concludes inner-membrane ATP synthesis merely slows rather than stops when the ETC is blocked)
**Distractor citations:**
  - Lines 9618 + 5574-5575 (Choice A, reversed relationship): cytochrome c oxidase is "a component of the electron transport chain" (respiration), and chloroplasts instead "carry out photosynthesis," so the enzyme is not the terminal enzyme of the chloroplast light reactions and the mitochondrion—not the chloroplast—is the affected organelle.
  - Lines 9618 + 5495 (Choice B, process-step confusion): cytochrome c oxidase is an inner-membrane electron-transport-chain component, not a cytosolic glycolytic enzyme, so the perturbation does act inside the mitochondrion and the organelles do not both continue normally.
  - Lines 9618 + 5495 (Choice C, partial truth): "ATP synthesis takes place on the inner membrane," which depends on the electron transport chain that cytochrome c oxidase completes; blocking the chain halts oxidative ATP synthesis even though the matrix citric-acid-cycle enzymes themselves are untouched, so ATP synthesis stops rather than continuing at a reduced rate.
**Hard-tier self-check:** Meets 3 of 4 gate conditions. (1) Concept-chaining (>=2 linked steps): step 1, locate cytochrome c oxidase as a mitochondrial inner-membrane electron-transport-chain component (line 9618); step 2, recognize that inner-membrane ATP synthesis (line 5495) collapses when the chain is blocked; step 3, contrast this with the chloroplast endosymbiont, which has the same double-membrane/own-circular-DNA headline but performs photosynthesis (lines 5574-5575, 5592) and therefore lacks this respiratory enzyme—three linked steps. (2) True partial-truth trap: Choice C is genuinely correct that the matrix citric-acid-cycle enzymes are not the direct target, and would be defensible if ATP synthesis could proceed without the ETC—it fails only because inner-membrane ATP synthesis requires the very chain cytochrome c oxidase completes. (3) 510-tempting distractor: Choice C punishes the strong test-taker who knows the citric acid cycle runs in the matrix and reasons 'matrix enzymes intact -> some ATP continues,' missing that the ETC, not the matrix enzymes, drives inner-membrane ATP synthesis. Wrong paths punished: A = reverses the organelle, assigning the respiratory enzyme to the chloroplast's light reactions; B = misplaces cytochrome c oxidase in cytosolic glycolysis so predicts no organelle is affected; C = stops at 'matrix enzymes untouched' and underestimates that blocking the ETC fully halts oxidative ATP synthesis.

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Cell and Tissue Structure (BIO)'. The answer is D because cytochrome \(c\) oxidase belongs to the mitochondrial inner-membrane electron-transport chain, so inactivating it halts mitochondrial ATP synthesis while leaving the chloroplast's photosynthetic light reactions—which do not use this enzyme—intact. Cytochrome \(c\) oxidase is "a component of the electron transport chain" (textbook line 9618), and in mitochondria "ATP synthesis takes place on the inner membrane" (line 5495), where the double-membrane, own-DNA "powerhouse" carries out cellular respiration to make ATP (lines 5474–5475, 5485). The chloroplast is the endosymbiotic near-twin—it too has "outer and inner membranes," "their own DNA and ribosomes," and "a single circular chromosome" (lines 5574, 5581, 5592)—but it has "an entirely different function," photosynthesis, which captures light energy in the thylakoid membranes (lines 5574–5575, 5590), not the respiratory electron-transport machinery that cytochrome \(c\) oxidase serves. The chain of reasoning is to locate cytochrome \(c\) oxidase in the respiratory ETC, recognize that only the mitochondrion runs that chain, and conclude the chloroplast's distinct photosynthetic pathway is spared. (Choice A) This reverses the organelle relationship: cytochrome \(c\) oxidase is a respiratory ETC enzyme of the mitochondrion (line 9618), not a chloroplast light-reaction enzyme, so the claim assigns the affected pathway to the wrong organelle and incorrectly spares the mitochondrion. (Choice B) Cytochrome \(c\) oxidase is not a cytosolic glycolytic enzyme; it is an inner-membrane component of the mitochondrial electron transport chain (lines 9618, 5495), so the perturbation does act inside the mitochondrion and the "both continue normally" prediction fails. (Choice C) This partial-truth trap is correct that the matrix citric-acid-cycle enzymes are not the direct target, but ATP synthesis depends on the inner-membrane electron-transport chain that cytochrome \(c\) oxidase completes (lines 9618, 5495); with the chain blocked, the proton gradient cannot be regenerated, so oxidative ATP synthesis stops rather than merely slowing. (Choice D) Correct: cytochrome \(c\) oxidase is a respiratory ETC component on the mitochondrial inner membrane where ATP synthesis occurs (lines 9618, 5495), and the chloroplast performs photosynthesis without this enzyme (lines 5574–5575), so only mitochondrial ATP synthesis stops. This is a Scientific Reasoning and Problem Solving question because you must predict the functional consequence of a targeted enzyme inactivation by tracing cytochrome \(c\) oxidase to the mitochondrial respiratory chain and distinguishing that pathway from the chloroplast's separate photosynthetic function.
