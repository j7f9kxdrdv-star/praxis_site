**Correct D** — OpenStax Biology 2e, file /Users/mikkonieveras/praxis-prep/.claude/worktrees/elegant-shaw/reference-textbooks/biology/openstax-biology-2e.txt, lines 5533-5534: "Each centriole is a cylinder of nine triplets of microtubules." (Choice C) The spindle is made of microtubules (polymerized α- and β-tubulin), not actin microfilaments; microtubules are what pull replicated chromosomes to opposite ends of a dividing cell. ## Q4 — Cytoskeleton: Cilia and Flagella — medium (topic:medium/struct:medium) — Skill 2 — ~110s — conf 5 (g35)
**Stem:** A swimming human sperm cell propels itself by beating its single flagellum, whose core is a 9+2 axoneme in which dynein motor arms anchored to each outer microtubule doublet step along the adjacent doublet, driving the two doublets to slide lengthwise relative to each other. The flagellum is anchored at its base, and nexin links cross-tie neighboring doublets along the shaft. Given these constraints, how does the dynein-driven sliding most directly produce the bending stroke that pushes the cell forward?

- A. Dynein hydrolyzes ATP to lengthen the central pair of microtubules, and this elongation telescopes the axoneme outward to create the propulsive stroke.
- B. Because the nexin links and basal anchor resist the dynein-generated sliding, the constrained shear between doublets is converted into localized bending of the axoneme. ✅
- C. Dynein detaches the outer doublets from one another so they slide freely, and the resulting telescoping elongation of the flagellum bends it into a propulsive curve.

**Correct B** — openstax-biology-2e.txt, lines 5932-5934: "Despite their differences in length and number, flagella and cilia share a common structural arrangement of microtubules called a “9 + 2 array.” ... a single flagellum or cilium is made of a ring of nine microtubule doublets, surrounding a single microtubule doublet in the center"; lines 5908-5909: microtubules "provide a track along which vesicles move through the cell" (the track motor proteins such as dynein walk); line 5924-5925: "The flagella ... are long, hair-like structures that extend from the plasma membrane and enable an entire cell to move (for example, sperm ...)."
**Distractors:** A=process_step_confusion (Invents central-pair elongation and frames the output as telescoping rather than bending); C=reversed_relationship (Reverses the role of the constraint: claims free (unresisted) sliding produces the bend); D=misconception (Maps the actomyosin sliding-filament/contraction model onto dynein, treating it as intrafilament shortening)
**Distractor citations:**
  - Choice A — refuted by lines 5905-5910, which describe microtubules as fixed-structure hollow tubes that serve as tracks and "resist compression"; the text gives no ATP-driven lengthening of the central pair, and line 5924 frames flagellar action as movement/beating, not telescopic elongation.
  - Choice C — refuted by the same 9+2 structural description (lines 5933-5934): the doublets form an integrated ring array, not freely dissociating units; the prompt's nexin links and basal anchor (consistent with the array being a fixed structural arrangement) mean sliding is constrained, not free.
  - Choice D — refuted by lines 5871-5874, where the actin/myosin sliding mechanism is described as filaments that "slide past each other" causing contraction in muscle; this is the model being mis-mapped onto dynein, and line 5919 lists microtubules (not actin/myosin) as the structural elements of flagella, so a myosin-style intrafilament shortening does not apply.

**Explanation:** This is a Biology question that falls under the content category 'Cell and Tissue Structure (BIO)'. The answer is B because the bend arises only when the sliding force dynein generates between doublets is opposed by structural constraints, so the shear is redirected into curvature rather than lengthwise displacement. The flagellum and cilium share a 9+2 array: the text states that "a single flagellum or cilium is made of a ring of nine microtubule doublets, surrounding a single microtubule doublet in the center," and microtubules "provide a track along which vesicles move" — the same tracks that motor proteins like dynein walk along. Dynein arms anchored on one doublet step toward the minus end of the neighboring doublet, which by itself would simply make adjacent doublets slide past one another. Because the nexin links cross-tie the doublets and the basal body anchors the base, free sliding is prevented; the resisted shear instead forces the axoneme to bend locally, and waves of such bending propagate down the flagellum to generate forward thrust (cellular locomotion, the listed function of flagella). (Choice A) The central pair does not elongate by ATP-driven lengthening, and axonemal motion is bending, not telescopic elongation — this misattributes both the mechanism and the resulting motion. (Choice B) Correct: the structural resistance (nexin links plus the basal anchor) converts interdoublet sliding into bending, which is the propulsive stroke. (Choice C) If dynein let the doublets slide freely, the axoneme would mostly telescope/elongate rather than bend; the whole point of the nexin links is that sliding is constrained, so unconstrained free sliding cannot produce the bend. (Choice D) Dynein does not shorten an individual microtubule the way myosin pulls on actin in a sarcomere; it walks along an adjacent doublet to create sliding between two filaments, not contraction within one. This is a Scientific Reasoning and Problem Solving question because you must combine the axoneme's structure with the behavior of its motor and anchoring proteins to predict how a sliding force is transformed into a bending stroke.
