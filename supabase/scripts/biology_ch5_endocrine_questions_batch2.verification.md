### Q1 — Hormone Transport, Specificity & Kinetics (medium, Skill 2)
**Stem:** A lipophilic hormone circulates reversibly attached to a plasma protein, in equilibrium with a small fraction dissolved directly in plasma. A patient is given a drug that acutely doubles the plasma concentration of this binding protein. Before any change in glandular secretion occurs, what happens to the amount of hormone able to enter and act on target cells?  
**Answer:** A · **Foundation:** 3A · **Confidence:** 5  
**Answer citation:** OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology 2e, Ch. 17: 'In the bloodstream, less than one percent of the circulating T3 and T4 remains unbound. This free T3 and T4 can cross the lipid bilayer of cell membranes and be taken up by cells. The remaining 99 percent... is bound to specialized transport proteins called thyroxine-binding globulins (TBGs), to albumin, or to other plasma proteins. This packaging prevents their free diffusion into body cells.' Only the free fraction is active; adding binding protein shifts the reversible equilibrium toward the bound state, transiently lowering free hormone.  
**Distractor citation 1:** Choice B — OpenStax A&P 2e Ch. 17 states it is the free (unbound) hormone, not the total, that crosses the lipid bilayer into cells ('This free T3 and T4 can cross the lipid bilayer... The remaining 99 percent... prevents their free diffusion into body cells'); a higher total or higher bound pool does not raise the active fraction.  
**Distractor citation 2:** Choice C — OpenStax A&P 2e Ch. 17: the active pool is the unbound fraction, and bound hormone is released only 'when blood levels... begin to decline,' so the free active fraction is not fixed when binding-protein levels change even if total is momentarily constant.  
**Distractor citation 3:** Choice D — OpenStax A&P 2e Ch. 17: transport proteins provide 'packaging' that 'prevents their free diffusion into body cells'; steroid hormones 'readily diffuse through the lipid bilayer' only as free hormone, so carriers retain hormone in blood rather than delivering it into cells.  

### Q2 — Hormone Transport, Specificity & Kinetics (medium, Skill 2)
**Stem:** A peptide hormone secreted into the blood reaches a person's hepatocytes and adipocytes at identical concentrations, yet only the hepatocytes increase their glucose release in response. Which factor best explains why the adipocytes fail to respond?  
**Answer:** B · **Foundation:** 3A · **Confidence:** 5  
**Answer citation:** OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology 2e, Ch 17.2 Hormones: 'Although a given hormone may travel throughout the body in the bloodstream, it will affect the activity only of its target cells; that is, cells with receptors for that particular hormone.' Also 'target cells must have receptors specific to a given hormone if that hormone is to trigger a response.' OpenStax Biology 2e, 37.2 How Hormones Work: 'even though hormones circulate throughout the body and come into contact with many different cell types, they only affect cells that possess the necessary receptors.'  
**Distractor citation 1:** Choice A: OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology 2e, Ch 17.2 (Pathways Involving Cell Membrane Hormone Receptors): 'Hydrophilic, or water-soluble, hormones are unable to diffuse through the lipid bilayer of the cell membrane and must therefore pass on their message to a receptor located at the surface of the cell... all amino acid–derived hormones bind to cell membrane receptors' — peptide hormones act at the cell surface, so tissue selectivity is not a matter of membrane entry/solubility, and the claim that a peptide hormone is 'too lipid-soluble to enter' is backwards.  
**Distractor citation 2:** Choice C: OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology 2e, Ch 17.2 (Factors Affecting Target Cell Response): hormones can interact via permissive, synergistic, and antagonistic effects (e.g., insulin vs. glucagon) that modulate the magnitude of a target cell's response — but such interactions modify responses in cells that already have receptors; they do not create tissue specificity or explain a complete lack of response.  
**Distractor citation 3:** Choice D: OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology 2e, Ch 17.2 (Regulation of Hormone Secretion) and OpenStax Biology 2e 37.2: receptor binding initiates the response, and the presence/number of receptors determines whether and how strongly a cell responds; hormone degradation/clearance affects circulating hormone levels and duration of action, not whether a receptor-lacking cell can respond to hormone that reaches it at equal concentration.

**Distractor citation 3:** (D) OpenStax A&P 2e Ch 17.2 (lines 31502–31505): the amino acid–derived epinephrine (~1 min) is the rapidly cleared hormone; rapid degradation produces a short, not long, half-life, contradicting Hormone Y's 60–90 minute value.

### Q10 — Feedback & Neuroendocrine Integration (medium, Skill 2)
**Stem:** During labor, stretch of the cervix signals the pituitary to secrete a hormone that strengthens uterine contractions, which stretch the cervix still further and evoke yet more of that same hormone. **Answer:** D · **Foundation:** 3A · **Confidence:** 5

**Distractor citation 2:** (Choice C) OpenStax A&P 2e, Ch 1 & Ch 17: negative feedback loops maintain a variable within a narrow range/set point by correcting deviations, whereas positive feedback 'intensifies a change... the system moves farther away from the normal range' — the described cascade has no stabilizing set point.  
**Distractor citation 3:** (Choice A) OpenStax A&P 2e, Ch 1: positive feedback produces 'more change, and the system moves farther away from the normal range,' rather than a response that reverses its triggering stimulus; reversing the stimulus to restore the resting state characterizes negative feedback.
